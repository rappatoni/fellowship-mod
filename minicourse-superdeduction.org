Eight lessons on two papers:

- [H] Clément Houtmann, /Superdeduction in λ̄μμ̃/, CL&C 2010, EPTCS 47,

* Lesson S1 - Theories as rewrite rules

** Definition

A first-order theory usually comes as axioms. Superdeduction starts from a
different presentation: a set ℛ of *proposition rewrite rules*

:   r : P → φ

where P is an *atomic* proposition and φ an arbitrary formula. The rule
stands for the axiom ∀x̄.(P ⇔ φ), x̄ the free variables of P. It reads as
a definition: P is, by definition, φ.

There are three ways to reason with such a theory.

1. *Compatible theory.* Add the axioms ∀x̄.(P ⇔ φ) and use plain LK or
   NJ. Every use of the definition costs a cut on the axiom and a
   decomposition of φ.
2. *Deduction modulo* (Dowek, Hardin, Kirchner 2003). Identify P and φ:
   every inference rule applies modulo the congruence ≡ generated by ℛ.
   Rewriting happens silently, and P disappears from proofs.
3. *Superdeduction* (Brauner, Houtmann, Kirchner 2007). Precompute, for
   each rule, the inferences that decomposing φ would take, and add them
   to the calculus as new rules about P. P stays in the proof, as the
   conclusion of a rule whose name is r.

The three prove the same sequents, under conditions the later lessons make
precise. [B] cites Dowek, Hardin and Kirchner for the equivalence between
deduction modulo and the compatible theory. Burel's Corollary 5 gives the
same equivalence for LB⁺, his intuitionistic super sequent calculus.

The motivating example of both papers is inclusion:

:   A ⊆ B → ∀x.(x ∈ A ⇒ x ∈ B)

In natural deduction modulo the proof of A ⊆ B ends with ∀-introduction
applied to a formula that is not a ∀ until you unfold it. The superdeduction
rule makes the step explicit and named:

:   Γ, x ∈ A ⊢ x ∈ B
:   ──────────────── ⊆I     (x not free in Γ)
:   Γ ⊢ A ⊆ B

** Worked example

lib/fol.fspy is a compatible theory. Its rules are axioms, and every use
costs a =cut= on the axiom, an =axiom= step and one =elim= per
quantifier and implication. The first probe proof, =all_ex=, spends 14
tactic lines on two rule applications. A superdeduction calculus would
write each application as one step.

** Connection to AC/DC

A rule P → φ is a *named piece of argument structure*. Lesson S3 shows
that its super rules have exactly the shape of a hyperedge in the document
graph: one target statement, n sources. Today the compiler recovers edges
by matching lambda and scaffold shapes in a decomposed term. With
superdeduction the edge is the rule.

** Exercises

1. Write the compatible theory of =⊆= as two Fellowship declarations.
   Fellowship has no ⇔, so you need two implications.
2. Why must the left-hand side P be atomic? What would a rule
   =A ∧ B → C= mean, and why is it not a definition of anything?

* Lesson S2 - Computing super rules in LK

** Definition

[H, §1] For a rule r : P → φ:

- *Right rule.* Decompose the sequent ⊢ φ bottom-up with the rules of LK
  minus Cut and the contractions, until only atomic sequents
  (Γᵢ ⊢ Δᵢ)₁≤ᵢ≤ₙ remain. Each ∀R and ∃L used on the way contributes the
  side condition "x ∉ FV(Γ, Δ)". The right rule is

  :   (Γ, Γᵢ ⊢ Δᵢ, Δ)ᵢ
  :   ──────────────── C
  :   Γ ⊢ P, Δ

- *Left rule.* Decompose φ ⊢ in the same way into (Γ'ⱼ ⊢ Δ'ⱼ)ⱼ and side
  conditions C'. The left rule is

  :   (Γ, Γ'ⱼ ⊢ Δ'ⱼ, Δ)ⱼ
  :   ───────────────── C'
  :   Γ, P ⊢ Δ

Decomposition is non-deterministic, because the order of rule applications
matters for quantifiers. Several rules can come out, and the complete
superdeduction system adds all of them [H, Definition 1].

Known results quoted by [H]:

- Superdeduction systems are always *sound* with respect to predicate logic.
- They are *complete* when right-hand sides do not alternate quantifiers.
  (∀x.φ) ∧ (∃y.ψ) is allowed. An ∃ under a ∀ is not.
- *Cut elimination* is harder. When no right-hand side contains both ∀ and
  ∃, cut elimination in superdeduction is equivalent to cut elimination in
  deduction modulo (Houtmann 2008). Counterexamples exist otherwise
  (Houtmann's thesis).

** Worked example: inclusion, decomposed by Fellowship

Superdeduction's decomposition is ordinary proof search, so the kernel can
compute it. Give Fellowship the right-hand side as a goal and apply =elim=
until only atoms are left (=s2_inclusion_right.fsp=):

: theorem rhs : (forall x:El, mem [x] [A] -> mem [x] [B]).
: elim.
: elim h.

Fellowship stops with one open goal and the partial proof term

: x:El
: h:mem x A
:  |-----  ?1.1.1
: *:mem x B
:
: ;:thesis:forall x:El,mem x A->mem x B.<\x:El.\h:mem x A.?1.1.1||thesis>

The open goal is the premise of the right rule, the eigenvariable x is its
side condition, and the partial term is the *interpretation* of the rule in
Houtmann's sense (lesson S4): [H] writes it λx.λx.μα.X, using the same
letter for the first-order variable and the hypothesis.

The left rule decomposes the definition as a hypothesis
(=s2_inclusion_left.fsp=). The =cut= / =axiom H= pair puts the hypothesis
in focus, and then =elim [t]= instantiates the quantifier at a term t:

: ;:thesis:(forall x:El,mem x A->mem x B)->C.<\H:forall x:El,mem x A->mem x B.;:th:C.<H||t*?1.1.2.1.1*1.1.2.1.2?>||thesis>

Two open goals remain: =?1.1.2.1.1=, a proof of =mem t A=, and
=1.1.2.1.2?=, a context with =mem t B= on the left. Read the context
=t*?1.1.2.1.1*1.1.2.1.2?= against [H]'s interpretation t·(μβ.Y)·(μ̃y.Z):
the term t, a proof owed, and a refutation owed. They are the two premises
of

:   Γ, t ∈ B ⊢ Δ      Γ ⊢ t ∈ A, Δ
:   ───────────────────────────── ⊆L
:   Γ, A ⊆ B ⊢ Δ

** Connection to AC/DC

Fellowship's metavariables are AIDA's open sites. A super rule is
therefore a *schematic enthymeme*: an argument for P, or against P, whose
open sites are the premises. =?n= is a term-side site and =n?= a
context-side site, the two sides of lesson 1 of =minicourse.org=. Which
*kind* each site has, obligation or presumption, is not part of the rule.
It is chosen when the rule is used, as =by default= chooses it today.

** Exercises

1. Decompose =A ⊆ B= on the right in the other possible order. Is there
   one? Why can the order not matter here?
2. Compute the super rules for =A = B → A ⊆ B ∧ B ⊆ A=, treating =⊆= as
   atomic. How many premises does the right rule have? The left rule?
3. Then unfold =⊆= too, with the rule of this lesson. Why is the result
   different from composing the two rule sets?

* Lesson S3 - A rule set from logic programming

** Definition

A normal logic program is a set of clauses =p :- body=. Its *Clark
completion* turns all clauses with head p into one definition

:   p ⇔ body₁ ∨ … ∨ bodyₖ

and that is a proposition rewrite rule p → body₁ ∨ … ∨ bodyₖ. Negation as
failure in a body becomes ¬ in φ.

** Worked example: tweety

=tests/fixtures/tweety.pl= in the scasp-aida repository contains the clause

: fly(X) :- bird(X), not abnormal(X).

Its completion, ground at tweety and with Fly as the defined atom, is the
rule Fly → Bird ∧ ¬Abnormal. Fellowship computes the right rule
(=s3_fly_right.fsp=):

: ;:thesis:Bird/\~Abnormal.<(?1.1,;:H1:~Abnormal.<\H2:Abnormal.;:H3:false.<H2||1.2.1?>||H1>)||thesis>

with the open goals =?1.1= (prove =Bird=) and =1.2.1?= (=Abnormal= on
the left, nothing to prove: refute it). So

:   Γ ⊢ Bird, Δ      Γ, Abnormal ⊢ Δ
:   ──────────────────────────────── Fly_R
:   Γ ⊢ Fly, Δ

The left rule (=s3_fly_left.fsp=) leaves one premise of the rule,
=?1.1.2.1.2.1=, with =hb:Bird= among the hypotheses and =Abnormal= to
prove. The second goal, =1.1.2.2?=, has only =C ⊢ C= left and is an
artefact of the =cut= that put the hypothesis in focus, closed by =axiom=.

:   Γ, Bird ⊢ Abnormal, Δ
:   ───────────────────── Fly_L
:   Γ, Fly ⊢ Δ

** Connection to AC/DC

- *Negation as failure is a refutation site.* =not abnormal= is not a
  proof of something negative. It is a context-side premise: Abnormal must
  be refutable. naf_olon.fspy builds the same thing by hand: "the
  term-side R->false that qRule needs is derived from a geled for R", a
  context-side presumption. Fly_R produces that shape from the program.
- *The left rule is the counterargument's recipe.* Fly_L says that to use
  Fly against someone, assume Bird and establish Abnormal, which is
  exactly where the penguin argument attacks. Superdeduction derives both
  directions of the debate from one clause.
- *A rule application is a hyperedge.* Fly_R has two premises with sides
  term and context, so its edge has two sources, Bird[t] and
  Abnormal[c]. The s(CASP) importer currently curries a multi-atom body
  into nested implications (=f_Bird_Tweety_Fly_Tweety= in tweety.fspy),
  which is what the waiting task on curried implications is about.
- *Disjunctions are alternatives.* A program with two clauses for p
  completes to body₁ ∨ body₂, and LK's ∨R keeps both disjuncts as two
  conclusions. Compare the support scaffold, which is also two
  alternatives for one statement.

** Exercises

1. Compute both super rules for =Abnormal → Penguin=. Then compose them
   with Fly_L. Which argument of tweety.pl do you get?
2. The program =p :- not p.= completes to P → ¬P. Compute its two super
   rules. Keep them for lesson S5.
3. Two clauses =p :- q.= and =p :- r.= complete to P → Q ∨ R. Compute
   the right and left rules. How many premises does each have, and on
   which sides?

* Lesson S4 - Proof terms: super terms in λ̄μμ̃

** Definition

[H, Figure 2] uses this λ̄μμ̃:

: c ::= ⟨π|e⟩                                      commands
: π ::= x | λx.π | μα.c | λx.π                     terms (the last λ is first-order)
: e ::= α | π·e | μ̃x.c | t·e | f                   environments (f realises falsity)

with the reductions

: ⟨λx.π | π'·e⟩ → ⟨π[π'/x] | e⟩
: ⟨μα.c | e⟩    → c[e/α]
: ⟨π | μ̃x.c⟩    → c[π/x]
: ⟨λx.π | t·e⟩  → ⟨π[t/x] | e⟩

Fellowship's =t3rm=, =context= and =command= in core.ml are this
grammar, with conjunction, disjunction and existential constructors added.

*Traces.* Totally order the quantifier inferences of a (possibly
unfinished) derivation. Each ∀R or ∃L creates an eigenvariable, written
x?. Each ∀L or ∃R instantiates with a term, written t!. The resulting list
is a *trace*. It fixes which instantiations may mention which
eigenvariables.

*Super terms.* For r : P → φ with right premises (Γᵢ ⊢ Δᵢ)ᵢ and a trace L,
the right rule becomes the typing rule

:   (cᵢ ▷ Γ, x̄ᵢ:Γᵢ ⊢ ᾱᵢ:Δᵢ, Δ)ᵢ
:   ───────────────────────────────── rR  (side conditions C)
:   Γ ⊢ r(L, (μᵢ(x̄ᵢ, ᾱᵢ).cᵢ)ᵢ) : P | Δ

Each premise is a command cᵢ that binds its own hypotheses x̄ᵢ and
conclusions ᾱᵢ. The left rule dually gives an environment
r(L', (μ̃ⱼ(ȳⱼ, β̄ⱼ).c'ⱼ)ⱼ) : P ⊢. For inclusion:

: right:  r(x?, μ(x, α).c)                  c ▷ Γ, x : x∈A ⊢ α : x∈B, Δ
: left:   r(t!, μ̃₁(y).c₁, μ̃₂(β).c₂)          c₁ ▷ Γ, y : t∈B ⊢ Δ    c₂ ▷ Γ ⊢ β : t∈A, Δ

*Open λ̄μμ̃ and interpretations.* An open derivation is typed with
*command variables* X, Y, … standing for unproven leaves, each with a
full sequent as its type. Substitution into them is delayed. The
*interpretation* of a super term is a chosen ordinary open term whose
leaves are the command variables, one per premise, and whose trace is L.
For inclusion [H] chooses

: π = λx.λx.μα.X              e = t·(μβ.Y)·(μ̃y.Z)

** Why traces: the pathological example

[H, end of §2] considers r : P → (∃x₁∀x₂.A(x₁,x₂)) ∨ (∃y₁∀y₂.B(y₁,y₂)). Its
two most general right rules have the same premise Γ ⊢ A(t,x₂), B(u,y₂), Δ
and differ only in side conditions: either x₂ ∉ FV(Γ,Δ,u) and
y₂ ∉ FV(Γ,Δ), or x₂ ∉ FV(Γ,Δ) and y₂ ∉ FV(Γ,Δ,t). The proof-term syntax
of the original paper [Brauner, Houtmann, Kirchner 2007] wrote both as
rR(λx₂.λy₂.(λα.λβ.m), t, u, γ), so the term did not say which
instantiation may see which eigenvariable. The traces u!y₂?t!x₂? and
t!x₂?u!y₂? do, and the two terms then behave differently under cut
elimination.

** Worked example

Lesson S2 already showed Fellowship computing interpretations. The
partial term =\x:El.\h:mem x A.?1.1.1= is π with the metavariable
=?1.1.1= in place of μα.X, and the context =t*?1.1.2.1.1*1.1.2.1.2?= is e
with =?1.1.2.1.1= in place of μβ.Y and =1.1.2.1.2?= in place of μ̃y.Z.
Fellowship's metavariables are typed by full sequents, the goals it
prints, exactly as Houtmann's command variables are.

** Connection to AC/DC

- *Command variables are sites.* Houtmann needs open λ̄μμ̃ for the
  interpretations. AIDA has had open λ̄μμ̃ since its first enthymemes: its
  four kinds of site are typed metavariables. Grafting, citation and
  unfolding are all "substitute a term for a site", which is how [H]
  instantiates an interpretation.
- *A super term is a citation with arguments.* =cite NAME= puts a name at
  a site, standing for a registered term. A super term puts a rule name
  there, standing for the rule's interpretation, and adds the premises.
  "Names are transparent" in the README's sense becomes "super terms are
  transparent": expanding every super term gives an ordinary term.
- *Traces matter only first-order.* The debate fragment is
  quantifier-free, so a propositional first stage needs no traces at all.

* Lesson S5 - The supercut and strong normalisation

** Definition

A *supercut* is a cut between a right and a left super term of the same
rule:

: ⟨ r(L, (μᵢ(x̄ᵢ,ᾱᵢ).cᵢ)ᵢ) | r(L', (μ̃ⱼ(ȳⱼ,β̄ⱼ).c'ⱼ)ⱼ) ⟩

[H] reduces it in two steps. First, take the interpretations π and e and
normalise the ordinary cut ⟨π|e⟩ in open λ̄μμ̃, giving a command c over
the command variables. Then plug in the actual premises:

: ⟨ r(…) | r(…) ⟩  →  c[(cᵢ/Xᵢ)ᵢ, (c'ⱼ/Yⱼ)ⱼ]

Because P is atomic and only axioms and super rules introduce it, no
other new cut rule is needed.

*The catch.* ⟨π|e⟩ can have several normal forms, because λ̄μμ̃ has the
critical pair ⟨μα.c | μ̃x.c'⟩. For inclusion, ⟨λx.λx.μα.X | t·(μβ.Y)·(μ̃y.Z)⟩
has two:

: X{t/x}{(μβ.Y)/x}{μ̃y.Z/α}         and        Z{μα.X{t/x}{(μβ.Y)/x}/y}

so the supercut reduces to

: c[t/x][(μβ.c₂)/x][μ̃y.c₁/α]       or         c₁[μα.c[t/x][(μβ.c₂)/x]/y]

[H] fixes one interpretation per rule and so one reduct per rule.

*Results.*

- Theorem 1, subject reduction: for every ℛ, typing is preserved.
- Hypothesis 1: the rewriting relation of ℛ is weakly normalising and
  confluent, and no first-order function symbol occurs in a left-hand
  side.
- Theorem 2, strong normalisation: under Hypothesis 1, reduction is
  strongly normalising on well-typed terms.

The proof of Theorem 2 is a simulation. Expand every super term into its
interpretation and normalise every formula with ℛ. Each extended step
becomes one or more ordinary λ̄μμ̃ steps, and λ̄μμ̃ is strongly normalising
(Polonowski). The function-symbol condition is what makes normalising a
formula commute with substituting into it.

** Worked example: the critical pair in AIDA's normaliser

The propositional version of inclusion is the rule r : P → (A ⇒ B). Its
interpretations are π = λh.μa.X and e = (μb.Y)·(μ̃y.Z). Pick premises
that discard their binders, so that the two reducts really differ:

: X := <h:A || 4:A?>       the right premise: refutes A, ignores its conclusion a
: Y := <!2:A || b:A>       the left premise proving A
: Z := <!5:B || 3:B!>      the left premise using B, ignores its hypothesis y

Normalising the cut with AIDA's normaliser (=normalize_command= in
core/comp/oracle_terms.py):

: pi = λh:A.μa:B.<h:A||4:A?>
: e  = μb:A.<!2:A||b:A>*μ'y:B.<!5:B||3:B!>
: cbn -> !5:B || 3:B!
: cbv -> !2:A || 4:A?

Call-by-value gives Houtmann's first normal form, in which the right
premise survives with the proof of A plugged into h. Call-by-name gives
his second, in which the left premise survives.

** Connection to AC/DC

- *The supercut is a debate decision.* The two reducts mean "the
  definition's own derivation wins" and "the use of the definition wins".
  AIDA does not fix such a choice per rule. The witness labelling fixes it
  per scaffold (=minicourse-evaluation.org=, lesson 11). A
  supercut on unexpanded terms should therefore keep both reducts and let
  sigma choose, rather than adopt Houtmann's fixed interpretation.
- *Expanding first avoids the question.* If super terms are expanded
  before the strict, graph and sigma phases, the existing normaliser sees
  ordinary terms and makes its usual choices. That is the route of
  lesson S8, and it is also how the proof of Theorem 2 works.
- *Hypothesis 1 fails on loops, and argumentation picks up there.*
  Propositional rules, one per atom, are weakly normalising iff no
  definition depends on itself. The completion of naf_olon.fspy,
  P → Q, Q → ¬R, R → P, rewrites P to a formula containing ¬P and so
  violates it. So does every recursive program. Superdeduction's
  normalisation theorem covers the well-founded part of a program. The
  debate layer, with capture and labelling, handles the rest.
- *The function-symbol condition excludes recursion over data.* A rule
  like even(s(s(x))) → even(x) terminates but has a function symbol on
  the left. Theorem 2 does not cover it. This matters for any first-order
  stage, not for a propositional one.

* Lesson S6 - Intuitionistic superdeduction

** Definition

[B, §3] restricts to the connectives ⇒, ∧ and ∀.

*Supernatural deduction NJ⁺* (Wack). For a rule A → P, compute the
*introduction rule* by applying NJ's introduction rules bottom-up to P,
starred, while a starred formula remains; the open leaves are its
premises and A is its conclusion. Compute *elimination rules* by applying
elimination rules top-down from P*. All rules apply modulo the term
rewrite system.

*Why LB and not LJ.* Gentzen's LJ allows too few permutations between
inference rules, and a super sequent calculus built on it would be
incomplete even for simple theories. Burel uses LB (Waaler and Wallen),
which keeps several formulas on the right but discards them in ⇒R and ∀R.
That discarding is what makes it intuitionistic.

*LB⁺* computes left and right rules from LB as LK⁺ does from LK (lesson S2).

*Permutation-problem free formulas (ppf).* P ::= A | ⊤ | A ⇒ P | P ∧ P |
∀x.P, with A atomic: implications only have atomic antecedents. A rule is
ppf if its right-hand side is.

*Results.*

- Theorem 4: NJ⁺ proofs translate to LB⁺ proofs.
- Corollary 5: a sequent is provable in LB⁺ iff it is provable in LB plus
  the compatible theory.
- Proposition 3: for a ppf system and atomic Γ, Δ, Γ ⊢ Δ is provable in LB⁺
  iff it is provable with identity rules (Axiom and Cut) and super rules
  only.
- Theorem 6 and Corollary 7: then also in NJ⁺ with super rules and Axiom
  only.
- Cut is *not* admissible in LB⁺ (the Crab rule below).

** Worked example: Crab

Crab is A → B ∧ (A ⇒ C). It is ppf. Its NJ⁺ rules are

:   Γ ⊢ B    Γ, A ⊢ C              Γ ⊢ A              Γ ⊢ A    Γ ⊢ A
:   ───────────────── Crab_I       ───── Crab_E1      ──────────────── Crab_E2
:          Γ ⊢ A                   Γ ⊢ B                    Γ ⊢ C

and its LB⁺ rules are

:   Γ, B, C ⊢ Δ    Γ, B ⊢ A, Δ             Γ ⊢ B, Δ    Γ, A ⊢ C
:   ────────────────────────── Crab_L      ──────────────────── Crab_R
:          Γ, A ⊢ Δ                             Γ ⊢ A, Δ

B ⊢ C has no cut-free proof in LB⁺. In NJ⁺ it has a proof that uses only
super rules [B, Figure 5]: Crab_E2 applied to two copies of the proof of A
that Crab_I builds from B and from Crab_E2 under the hypothesis A.

In Fellowship, under =lj.= and with the compatible theory, the natural
proof factors out A ⇒ C as a lemma (=s6_crab.fsp=). Both theorems are
accepted:

: ;:thesis:A->C.<\hA:A.;:y:C.<crab_l||hA*(hB2:B,hAC:A->C).<;:z:C.<hAC||hA*z>||y>>||thesis>
: ;:thesis:B->C.<\hB:B.;:x:C.<ac||;:r:A.<crab_r||(hB,ac)*r>*x>||thesis>

The lemma =ac= is used twice in =bc=, once as the second component of the
pair and once applied to A. That is the cut Burel shows cannot be removed
from LB⁺.

With C := ⊥, Crab becomes A → B ∧ ¬A, the classic counterexample to cut
elimination in deduction modulo usually attributed to Crabbé.

** Connection to AC/DC

- *Arguments are intuitionistic, debates classical.* The COMMA paper
  defines an argument as a linear term and identifies the linear fragment
  with intuitionistic logic. Burel's NJ⁺ / LB⁺ is the superdeduction for
  arguments. Houtmann's classical LK⁺ is the superdeduction for debates.
  The pending task to restrict arguments to lj applies unchanged: a
  super rule computed in LB⁺ stays in the intuitionistic fragment.
- *Counterarguments need the dual.* Neither paper has dual-intuitionistic
  superdeduction. The prover has no dual-intuitionistic mode either (see
  the lj restriction task in tasks.org). This is an open point for AC/DC,
  not a gap in the papers.
- *Cut in the kernel is free.* Fellowship always has cut, so LB⁺'s
  missing cut admissibility does not affect checking. It does matter for
  a proof search, an =auto= or an importer, that tries to build arguments
  from super rules alone.
- *Crab is an odd loop.* A depends on itself under an implication. With
  C := ⊥ it is the propositional shape of =p :- b, not p=. Lesson S5's
  remark applies: the debate layer is where such definitions belong.

* Lesson S7 - Superdeduction as a logical framework

** Definition

A logical framework is a meta-language for specifying deductive systems
(Pfenning). LF does it with dependent types: an object logic becomes a
signature in λΠ. Burel goes the other way: an object logic becomes a
*first-order theory* presented as rewrite rules, and superdeduction
computes the object logic's own rules from it.

A *Pure Type System* ⟨S, A, R⟩ has sorts S, axioms A ⊆ S × S and rules
R ⊆ S × S × S, and covers the simply typed λ-calculus, λΠ, the calculus of
constructions and λHOL. Burel encodes every *functional* PTS.

*Terms.* PTS terms become first-order terms of a λ-calculus with
*explicit substitutions*, written with named variables. λ is a first-order
function symbol, and a substitution s is a first-order term too: shift,
lift(s), cons(t). Burel does not fix one calculus. He fixes a *scheme*
of 12 conditions (confluent, terminating, pure normal forms, …), which λσ
satisfies. Each rule ⟨s₁,s₂,s₃⟩ ∈ R gets a function symbol π for Π, and
each sort s a constant.

*Typing* is the predicate ε(a,b), "a has type b". Three proposition
rewrite rules simulate the typing rules:

: ε(s₁, s₂)                 → ⊤                                        ⟨s₁,s₂⟩ ∈ A    (1)
: ε(π(a,b), s₃)             → ε(a, s₁) ∧ ∀z. ε(z,a) ⇒ ε(b[cons(z)], s₂)                (2)
: ε(t, π(a,b))              → ε(π(a,b), s₃) ∧ ∀z. ε(z,a) ⇒ ε(t z, b[cons(z)])          (3)

with π indexed by ⟨s₁,s₂,s₃⟩ ∈ R in (2) and (3). β-conversion is not a
rule. It is the term rewrite system, and NJ⁺ works modulo it.

*Results.*

- Theorem 9, soundness: Γ ⊢ T : A in the PTS implies |Γ| ⊢⁺ ε(|T|, |A|).
- Theorem 10, conservativity: |Γ| ⊢⁺ ε(a, b) implies a →* |A|, b →* |B|
  and Γ ⊢ A : B for some A and B. Terms on the left of ε are only
  determined up to β, so nothing stronger holds.
- Abstraction corresponds to the introduction rule (3)_I and application
  to the elimination rule (3)_E2, so β-redexes of the PTS become redexes
  of NJ⁺.
- Appendix C: one π per rule is necessary. With a single π, the calculus
  of constructions would derive ε(⋆, ⋆), which leads to Girard's paradox.

** Worked example

Burel's Figure 1 places Fellowship at the top, as the system that
translates first-order sequent proofs into Coq, PVS and others. The
arrow from supernatural deduction back to Fellowship is the point of the
encoding: since NJ⁺ ≡ LB + compatible theory, a PTS typing proof becomes a
first-order proof that Fellowship can check.

Compare lib/fol.fspy, which encodes first-order logic Isabelle style:
=forallf : [termf -> form] -> form= uses higher-order abstract syntax and
leaves object-level binding to Fellowship's own quantifiers. Burel's
encoding has no binders in terms at all. Binding is done by explicit
substitution, which is plain first-order rewriting.

** Connection to AC/DC

- *This is the COMMA paper's future work line.* The paper's last
  sentence lists "a first order extension of the language and its use as a
  logical framework in the style of Isabelle". lib/fol.fspy is the
  Isabelle style. Burel is the superdeduction style. They differ in where
  binding lives.
- *What Fellowship lacks is the modulo.* Burel's calculus has no equality
  predicate either. It has conversion built into every rule. Fellowship
  has neither, so checking a PTS encoding there means replacing each
  silent conversion by an explicit equational proof (lesson S8).
- *Fellowship's terms are enough for the encoding.* Terms are
  applicative symbols without binders. Substituting for a propositional
  variable aborts the prover, and AIDA's parser rejects lambda binders of
  sort bool; both are recorded tasks. Burel's encoding needs only
  applicative first-order terms, so it avoids both, at the price of
  explicit substitutions.
- *Proposition identity.* The document graph identifies statements
  syntactically, up to canonical spelling. Modulo β, two different
  spellings are one statement. canonical_prop would have to normalise
  with the term rewrite system, which is only well defined when the
  system is confluent and terminating, as the scheme requires.

* Lesson S8 - A wrapper-side architecture for AIDA

This lesson is a proposal from the discussion of 2026-10-02/04, not a
result of either paper.

** Definition

All of superdeduction lives in the wrapper, and Fellowship checks only
ordinary first-order proofs in the compatible theory, as Burel's Figure 1
envisions.

1. =rule r : P := φ= declares the two implications of the compatible
   theory as axioms in Fellowship.
2. The wrapper computes the super rules by driving the kernel, as in
   lessons S2 and S3: decompose φ, read the open goals and the partial term
   from the machine payload, and close each rule as a theorem. One trace
   per rule, which resolves Houtmann's non-determinism by convention.
3. Super terms exist only in the Python AST, as one node kind per side
   with the rule name and the premises. Expansion is the rule theorem
   applied, with the premises plugged into its sites. That is constant
   size per use, and the existing tactic lowering and replay check it.
4. Reduction, strictness and labelling run on expanded terms, with no
   change. A supercut rule for unexpanded terms can come later (lesson S5).
5. The compiler reads a super node as a hyperedge directly (lesson S3).

*Term rewriting needs equality.* Rules on propositions need nothing new.
Rules on terms, as in Burel's PTS encoding, need conversions. Without a
modulo in the kernel, a wrapper command can generate the equality theory
of a signature: per sort, reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity; per
function symbol and argument position, congruence; per predicate symbol
and argument position, substitutivity; per term rewrite rule, its
equation. That is linear in the signature. Arrow-sorted arguments, as in
lib/fol.fspy's =forallf=, would need extensionality and should be refused.

*Cost.* Checking is linear in the size of the emitted rewrite derivations,
not in the size of the proof modulo. One silent conversion step can hide
many rewrite steps. Each step costs a congruence chain as deep as the
redex, stating the whole intermediate proposition. Proving each
conversion once as a lemma and citing it shares the cost.

*What the kernel does not certify.* Fellowship certifies soundness
relative to the compatible theory, as both papers do. Nobody checks
Hypothesis 1 or the consistency of the theory. For AIDA that division is
natural: the well-founded definitions are superdeduction, and the loops
are argumentation (lesson S5).

** Worked example

Lessons S2 and S3 are step 2 run by hand. The machine payloads behind
them carry the open goals as structured data:

: (goal (meta "1.1.1")(kind goal)(side rhs)(active-prop "mem x B")(hyps ((name "h")(prop "mem x A")(visible t))) ... (env ((name "x")(sort "El"))))

(an excerpt), which is everything a super rule needs: premise sequents, eigenvariables
and the interpretation. The wrapper already parses this payload for every
tactic it replays.

** Exercises

1. Sketch the AST nodes for a right and a left super term. Which existing
   node is closest?
2. Count the axioms the equality command would generate for
   lib/division.fspy's signature.
3. Which phase of the evaluation pipeline in the README would notice a
   super term first if expansion were forgotten? What would it report?
4. Write the =rule= declaration for Fly and the debate fixture that
   attacks it through Fly_L.

* Appendix - Solutions

** Lesson S1

1. =declare sub_r : (forall a:Set, forall b:Set, (forall x:El, mem [x] [a] -> mem [x] [b]) -> sub [a] [b]).=
   and =sub_l= with the implication reversed. The right super rule comes
   from =sub_r=, the left from =sub_l=.
2. A definition says what one atom means. With A ∧ B → C, the new left
   and right rules would be about A ∧ B, a formula LK already has rules
   for. A cut on A ∧ B could then be reduced by LK's ∧ rules or by the
   new rules, and the two need not agree. With an atomic left-hand side,
   only axioms and super rules introduce P, so the supercut is the only
   new cut (lesson S5).

** Lesson S2

1. There is none that is different. ∀R must come before ⇒R because the
   quantifier is outermost, and there is only one quantifier, so no
   eigenvariable condition can change.
2. A = B → A ⊆ B ∧ B ⊆ A, with ⊆ atomic: the right rule has two premises,
   Γ ⊢ A ⊆ B, Δ and Γ ⊢ B ⊆ A, Δ. The left rule has one,
   Γ, A ⊆ B, B ⊆ A ⊢ Δ.
3. Decomposition stops at atoms, and A ⊆ B is an atom even when another
   rule defines it. So the rule for = never looks inside ⊆. If you unfold
   ⊆ into the right-hand side first, the right rule for = gets the two
   ⊆I premises directly, Γ, x ∈ A ⊢ x ∈ B, Δ and Γ, y ∈ B ⊢ y ∈ A, Δ.
   The provable sequents are the same. The proofs differ: the composed
   version has the intermediate atoms A ⊆ B and B ⊆ A, which can be cut
   against, and the unfolded version does not.

** Lesson S3

1. Abnormal_R has the premise Γ ⊢ Penguin, Δ. In Fly_L, the premise
   Γ, Bird ⊢ Abnormal, Δ is closed by Abnormal_R from Γ, Bird ⊢ Penguin, Δ.
   This is the counterargument "tweety is a penguin, so abnormal, so the
   rule for fly does not apply".
2. P → ¬P. Right: decompose ⊢ ¬P to P ⊢, so the rule is Γ, P ⊢ Δ / Γ ⊢ P, Δ.
   Left: decompose ¬P ⊢ to ⊢ P, so the rule is Γ ⊢ P, Δ / Γ, P ⊢ Δ.
3. Right: LK's ∨R keeps both disjuncts, so one premise Γ ⊢ Q, R, Δ. Left:
   two premises Γ, Q ⊢ Δ and Γ, R ⊢ Δ. A refutation of P must refute both
   clauses.

** Lesson S4

1. From the right decomposition: r(_, μ(∅, ∅).c₁, μ(H2, ∅).c₂) with
   c₁ ▷ Γ ⊢ Bird, Δ and c₂ ▷ Γ, H2 : Abnormal ⊢ Δ, empty trace. The
   interpretation is the partial term of lesson S3 with the two
   metavariables as command variables. The left super term is
   r(_, μ̃(hb, β).c) with c ▷ Γ, hb : Bird ⊢ β : Abnormal, Δ.
2. Neither is derivable from the other. Each forbids an occurrence that
   the other allows: u may mention x₂ in the first and not in the second,
   and dually for t and y₂. That is why both belong to the complete system.
3. Propositional rules introduce no variables that can escape, so their
   order does not change what a premise can mention. Only the relative
   order of eigenvariable creation and instantiation does.

** Lesson S5

1. The λ rule fires first in both strategies:
   ⟨λh.μa.X | (μb.Y)·(μ̃y.Z)⟩ → ⟨μa.X[μb.Y/h] | μ̃y.Z⟩. This is the
   critical pair.
   - Call-by-name fires μ̃, substituting the unevaluated term for y:
     Z[μa.X'/y] = ⟨!5 || 3!⟩, because Z does not use y.
   - Call-by-value fires μ: X'[μ̃y.Z/a] = ⟨μb.⟨!2||b⟩ || 4?⟩, because X
     does not use a. One more μ step gives ⟨!2 || 4?⟩.
2. With X := ⟨r1 || h·a⟩, Y := ⟨!2 || b⟩ and Z := ⟨y || 3!⟩, every
   binder is used once:

   : cbn -> r1:A->B || μb2:A.<!2:A||b2:A>*3:B!
   : cbv -> r1:A->B || !2:A*μ'b1:B.<b1:B||3:B!>

   The two results differ only by η-expansions of =!2= and =3:B!=. The
   critical pair only becomes visible when a premise discards a binder,
   which is the affine case AC/DC uses for throw and catch.
3. A sketch, by analogy with Russell's paradox. Write r_R(x.c) for the
   right rule with premise c ▷ x : P ⊢ and r_L(β.c') for the left rule
   with premise c' ▷ ⊢ β : P. Let c := ⟨x | r_L(β.⟨x||β⟩)⟩ and
   δ := r_R(x.c), a proof of P. Then ⟨δ | r_L(β.⟨δ||β⟩)⟩ is a supercut.
   Reducing it with the reduct c[μβ.⟨δ||β⟩/x] and one μ step gives a
   supercut of δ against a left term that is the original one up to η.
   This is the superdeduction view of the odd loop.

** Lesson S6

1. Crab_I: start from Γ ⊢ (B ∧ (A ⇒ C))*. ∧I gives Γ ⊢ B* and
   Γ ⊢ (A ⇒ C)*; B is atomic, so Γ ⊢ B; ⇒I gives Γ, A ⊢ C*, so Γ, A ⊢ C.
   The conclusion is Γ ⊢ A. Crab_E1: ∧E₁ from (B ∧ (A ⇒ C))* gives B.
   Crab_E2: ∧E₂ gives (A ⇒ C)*, and ⇒E with a second proof of A gives C.
2. The right rule of A in LK⁺ comes from ∧R and ⇒R:
   Γ, B ⊢ C, Δ and Γ ⊢ D, Δ / Γ ⊢ A, Δ. Apply it to D ⊢ A, B with Δ := B:
   the premises D, B ⊢ C, B and D ⊢ D, B are both axioms. In LB, ⇒R
   discards Δ, so the first premise becomes D, B ⊢ C, which is not
   provable. B is not provable from D alone either.
3. Bird ∧ ¬Abnormal is Bird ∧ (Abnormal ⇒ ⊥). Its implication has an
   atomic antecedent, so it is ppf, provided ⊥ is counted as an atom.
   Burel's restricted language has no ⊥, so this is a modelling decision.

** Lesson S7

1. S = {⋆, □}, A = {⟨⋆, □⟩}, R = {⟨⋆, ⋆, ⋆⟩}. One π. Rule (1) is
   ε(⋆, □) → ⊤. Rules (2) and (3) with s₁ = s₂ = s₃ = ⋆.
2. ε(a, s₁) and, for a fresh z, the premise ε(z, a) ⊢ ε(b[cons(z)], s₂).
   These are the two premises of Product, with the binder of Π replaced by
   the fresh z and explicit substitution.
3. The PTS conversion rule lets terms on the left of ε change by β.
   Burel's note after Theorem 10: ⊢⁺ ε((λλ1)(λ11)⋆, □) is provable in
   NJ⁺, because the term β-reduces to ⋆ and ε(⋆, □) holds. No choice of
   type annotations makes the corresponding λΠ term typable.

** Lesson S8

1. Two nodes, one term and one context, each with a rule name, a trace
