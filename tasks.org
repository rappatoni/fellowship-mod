Open design question (raised by the author, 2026-09-16): what do the
manual verbs =attack= / =support= / =chain= mean once the document
graph exists? Under the spec's universal-substitution decision,
contrariness follows from proposition identity, so registering a
counterargument to Q already conflicts with every use of Q in the
document - the attack is implicit, and the verb adds no conflict the
graph does not already contain. Observations bearing on the answer:
- The graph is built *incrementally* as arguments are registered and
  operators applied (phase B2); it is not recompiled on evaluation.
  What is recomputed per evaluation is the labelling (one adf-bdd call
  over the graph).
- Scope is reachability from the issue. Non-interference guarantees
  that arguments not connected to the issue do not affect its label, so
  independent debates in one file do not interfere unless they share a
  proposition - and when they do, the universal-substitution decision
  says they should interact.
- Hence the verbs are redundant for *semantics*. Candidate roles, none
  of which changes a label: (i) naming - =attack d att host= names
  debate d with issue = host's conclusion; (ii) assertion - it checks
  that att really concludes the contrary of a site reachable in host
  and errors otherwise, so nobody believes an attack landed when the
  graph shows none; (iii) presentation - it fixes which scion unfolding
  uses when producing a term for display.
- The alternative - explicit scoping, a debate as a user-selected
  subgraph within which alone implicit attacks fire - contradicts the
  universal-substitution decision and should be rejected unless that
  decision is revisited.
To be decided before phase C starts.

Discovered 2026-09-15 while tutoring lesson 3. =evaluate_debate=
(core/comp/evaluate.py:177) always takes sigma := =grounded_labels(graph)=;
the mode then only picks the wing at each UNDEC *scaffold*, independently,

So the implementation is a *local approximation* of the specified
discipline, and issue M4 of the original critique - per-critical-pair
choices are locally greedy and may correspond to no extension - is not in
fact discharged by the code.

attacker" selects exactly the extension in which the proponent wins.

when several contested nodes are linked through edges, so that the local
choices could combine into an assignment no single extension realises.

*derivation*, and a strict rule applied to a defeasible premise is a
strict rule: the premise is what is defeasible. The compiler merges the
two into one edge and lets the premise decide its strictness, so no
strict edge with sources is ever emitted and the closure acts on the
empty set. The author's own construction shows what is lost: from a
strict refutation t of P and a defeasible t' for P from presumed Q via
the axiom Q->P, one *can* build an attack on t' at Q from t by
contraposition - the term binds the premise, mu' q.<rule || alpha>. The
compiled graph cannot represent it because the rule is not an edge.

Design decision (author, 2026-09-16): *every* implication contraposes,
delegated ones included. The earlier note here imported the ASPIC+
position that defeasible rules must not be transposed; that position is
rejected for this system. Read defeasible implication as evidential
support: if A is strong evidence for B then not-B is strong evidence for
not-A (if P(B|A) > P(B) then P(not-A|not-B) > P(not-A)). The stock
counterexamples ("man -> not bearded", so "bearded -> not man"?) are
base-rate neglect: learning that something is a man *raises* the
credence that it is bearded, so that rule was never a legitimate
defeasible implication and should not be modelled as one. Consequently
the strict/defeasible distinction affects only *default status* in the
labelling, never which transposals exist.

Terminology, fixed: there is no separate category of "rule" in this
calculus. An implication is a proposition; it is either declared
(strict, an axiom) or delegated (defeasible, a presumption node). The
contraposition fixture tests/contrapositive.fspy is exactly a
*delegated* implication A->B being contraposed: it compiles to the edge
(A-B)[context] <- (A->B)[term:presumption], deriving not-B -> not-A from
the delegation. That is defeasible contraposition in action, and it is
what this task must generalise.

Shape of a transposed delegated edge (verified on a hand-built graph):
using A->B (presumption) to derive B from presumed A, with a strict
refutation of B, the transposal A[c] <- B[c], (A->B)[t] carries the
refutation to A. Result: A[t] UNDEC, A[c] UNDEC, (A->B)[t] IN, B[c] IN.
UNDEC rather than OUT is correct: A is itself presumed, so after the
transposal A is *contested* - its own presumption on the term side
against the delegated modus tollens on the context side - and grounded
declines to decide (the mode policy of Layer 4 then resolves it).
Compare the strict case: a declared A->B gives a strict transposal,
which bypasses the guard and sinks A[t] to OUT outright. That is the
whole effect of strictness under this design: it decides whether the
contraposed attack is decisive or merely contesting. Contrast the fused
edge of today, under which A simply survives as IN.

Where the strict rules went. A strict axiom used inside an argument does
not become a strict edge. =pArg=, which derives P from a presumed Q via
the declared axiom =pRule : Q->P=, compiles to one edge
=P[t] <- Q[t:presumption]=, marked *defeasible* because of its premise.
The rule and its premise are fused, and the edge's strictness is decided
by the premise. The rule's transposal =Q[c] <- P[c]= (modus tollens) is
therefore never generated.

Consequence, measured (hand-built graphs, same session):

: as compiled today      P[t]=(Q[t] & ~P[c])   Q[t]=true   P[c]=(true|false)
:                        labels: P OUT, Q IN, P[c] IN
: with rule + transposal P[t]=(Q[t] | (Q[t] & ~P[c]))   Q[t]=(true & ~Q[c])
:                        Q[c]=(P[c] | false)   P[c]=(true|false)
:                        labels: P OUT, Q OUT, P[c] IN, Q[c] IN

With P strictly refuted, Q should fall; today it stays IN. The correct
graph is refused by the acyclicity check, and rightly so: an implication
edge and its own transposal always undermine each other, which is a
derivation cycle through contrariness. Every debate that uses an
implication therefore leaves the acyclic fragment once this task lands,
hence the block on =aida-cyclic-fragment= (author's decision: the
acyclic fragment is only the initial one).

Object negation. =canonical_prop= maps =~K=, =-K= and =K->false= to one
node, distinct from K, and nothing links the two: Q and ~Q both presumed
ground both IN (oracle agrees - the conditions are faithful, the defect
is in what the conditions omit). The strict derivations relating a
refutation of K to a proof of K->false exist in the calculus; the bridge
edges are an instance of the same strict-layer work.

The married-bachelor fixture passes only because its strict bridge is
built by hand with =kind="assumption"= sources, which no compiled term
can produce. Related: =aida-strict-refutation-edges= (declared
refutations are dropped; the same "strict references carry no side"
problem at the leaf level).

Found 2026-09-16 by the author, from an attack in
tests/minicourse/lesson5_labels.fspy that failed:
=attack d6 test d5= with =test= a counterargument to B->C, against =cArg=,
which derives C from A and B via the axiom abc : A->(B->C).

*Why the attack fails.* =cArg= never proves B->C. The two =elim= steps
build the elimination stack =?A * !B * r=, and B->C occurs only as the
type of that stack's tail - a *context*:

: A->B->C   context:Cons, term:DI
: B->C      context:Cons                 <- no term-side node
: C         context:ID,   term:Mu

A counterargument attacks term-side nodes, so =ThetaExpander= finds no
target and =Argument.attack= raises. Strictness and default status are
not the cause: an attack on the conclusion C (neither default nor
strict) succeeds. An argument *for* B->C does find the context node, but
the result is the proof fed into the stack as an alternative route; the
graph shows no conflict at all.

*The spec disagrees with this.* debate-graph-spec.org says theta-normal
form exposes one open obligation per logically progressing node. The
step that eliminates A is such a node, and its conclusion is B->C; the
implementation only exposes nodes that already exist in the term.

*Half 1 - theta-expansion (verified 2026-09-16).* Naming the partial
application turns the intermediate conclusion into a term:

: before :  < abc  ||  ?A * !B * r >
: after  :  < mu b:B->C.< abc || ?A * b >  ||  !B * r >

The rewritten term replays in Fellowship (instructions: cut (B -> C),
cut (A -> B -> C), axiom abc, elim, next, moxia b, elim, by default,
next, moxia r, ...), and once it is in this form the challenge to B->C
attacks it successfully.

*Half 2 - the compiler (verified 2026-09-16).* Naming is not enough.
=compile_debate= still emits a single edge for the whole argument,
C[t] <- A[t], B[t], so the intermediate conclusion is not a node on the
path to C. With A and B presumed and a strict refutation of B->C
attacking the rewritten term:

: edges  : B->C[c] <- (none, strict)      C[t] <- A[t], B[t]
: labels : B->C[c] IN,   C[t] IN

The attack lands and is accepted, yet C is untouched. The spec requires
the presentation to be "closed under subarguments (every subargument of a
registered argument contributes its own hyperedge)"; the compiler only
emits edges for whole arguments (the host and each scion). It must emit
C[t] <- B->C[t], B[t] and B->C[t] <- A[t].

*Why it is gnarly* (the author's word):

- A curried implication with n premises has n-1 intermediate
  conclusions. Expanding every one is linear and deterministic (one mu
  per elimination step), but =ThetaExpander= is driven by the issue
  proposition, so it has to find the right prefix of the spine - the one
  whose type is the issue - and split the stack exactly there.
- The same implication may be applied several times, and the same
  intermediate type may occur on several spines; which occurrences are
  targets is the same universal-substitution question as for leaf sites.
- The split interacts with =aida-strict-layer=: once the implication is
  an edge of its own, each elimination step becomes an implication edge
  with one premise, and each of those has a transposal. Designing the
  two together avoids doing the spine decomposition twice.
- The first-order analogue is the instantiation spine: a partially
  instantiated =forall x, A x -> B x= has intermediate conclusions too
  (=ConsFO= in the stack), so the same splitting is needed once
  =aida-first-order-reduction= opens that fragment.
- The compiler must recognise the named partial application as a
  subargument (a mu-bound term with a proposition, not a scaffold),
  which it currently has no shape for.

*** Issues and suggested fixes

- [ ] /Stepwise protocol despite batching./ Quiet replay still performs one
  send, prompt wait, and response parse for every tactic. Introduce a
  prover-side =check_term= or framed bulk-replay request that returns the
  failing step or one final state without prompt-oriented PTY traffic.
- [ ] /Full proof-state snapshots./ Non-quiet replay repeatedly pretty-prints,
  hashes, and serializes the growing proof term, goals, and declarations. Keep
  quiet intermediate responses and emit at most one full final snapshot.
- [ ] /Proof-term reverse compilation./ Registration parses a proof term,
  translates it into tactics, replays those tactics, and then retains the
  original AST. Add a direct proof-term parser/checker accepting one structured
  certificate; retain tactic replay only for interactive proof construction.
- [ ] /Repeated checking of growing compositions./ Chaining and debate
  construction replay the entire combined term, so repeated composition can
  recheck increasingly large prefixes. Represent compositions as immutable
  DAGs or certificates, cache checks by term and environment hash, and verify
  only the new composition boundary where the calculus permits it.
