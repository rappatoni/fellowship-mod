Found on the way (verified against the binary):
- Fellowship fixes the logic at the first instruction and refuses a later
  toggle with a *message*; the wrapper had recorded the switch and
  cleared its document anyway, so the two disagreed.
- The wrapper's declarations survived =lk.=: stale names stayed strict.
- =discard all.= resets Fellowship (signature, theorems, moxias) and
  reopens the logic switch, but keeps the minimal/lj flags.

Design as built:
- =Document= holds logic and minimal, the head flag, declarations,
  decorations, names, arguments, graph, debates, the recording debate,
  anon names, revision (monotonic across documents) and the caches.
- =ProverWrapper= holds the process, custom tactics, the settings (read
  from the env once), a =threading.RLock= around every send, and =doc=;
  =prover.graph= is the document graph (was =prover.document=).
- =new_document(logic, minimal)= sends =discard all.=, =full.=/=minimal.=,
  =lk.=/=lj.=, and checks Fellowship's "Current logic:" reply.
- A toggle at the head sets the logic from Fellowship's reply; after it, a
  change raises ProverError and the logic in force is a no-op.
- Debate commands are refused in lj (as before) and in minimal logic
  ([[id:aida-minimal-logic-debates]]).
- Concurrency: one process per session, the lock serialises its I/O;
  compound operations take the lock in the service layer (Phase 3); what
  stays module-level is pure memoisation; =explain= redirects global
  loggers and is not safe concurrently (CLI-only).

- A theorem is a statement, not a witness: `theorem foo : A` registers

  refines it in place.

  argument by proposition identity anyway, but concretely it is an operation:
  the citing argument IS the term with the cited one grafted in
  intuitionistically, as the debate verbs are concrete operations over a
  timeless framework. Hence two bodies: the concrete one, and the atomic one
  the document compiles.

Why the closure has nothing to act on today - corrected 2026-09-16.
The earlier note (and the docstring in core/comp/adf_label.py) called
the closure "vacuous in the fragment" as though that were a property of
the fragment. It is not; it is a symptom of the rule/premise fusion
described below. The spec's =strict(e)= is a property of the
*derivation*, and a strict rule applied to a defeasible premise is a
strict rule: the premise is what is defeasible. The compiler merges the
two into one edge and lets the premise decide its strictness, so no
strict edge with sources is ever emitted and the closure acts on the
empty set. The author's own construction shows what is lost: from a
strict refutation t of P and a defeasible t' for P from presumed Q via
the axiom Q->P, one *can* build an attack on t' at Q from t by
contraposition - the term binds the premise, mu' q.<rule || alpha>. The
compiled graph cannot represent it because the rule is not an edge.

Design decision (author, 2026-09-16): *every* implication contraposes,
delegated ones included. The earlier note here imported the ASPIC+
position that defeasible rules must not be transposed; that position is
rejected for this system. Read defeasible implication as evidential
support: if A is strong evidence for B then not-B is strong evidence for
not-A (if P(B|A) > P(B) then P(not-A|not-B) > P(not-A)). The stock
counterexamples ("man -> not bearded", so "bearded -> not man"?) are
base-rate neglect: learning that something is a man *raises* the
credence that it is bearded, so that rule was never a legitimate
defeasible implication and should not be modelled as one. Consequently
the strict/defeasible distinction affects only *default status* in the
labelling, never which transposals exist.

Terminology, fixed: there is no separate category of "rule" in this
calculus. An implication is a proposition; it is either declared
(strict, an axiom) or delegated (defeasible, a presumption node). The
contraposition fixture tests/contrapositive.fspy is exactly a
*delegated* implication A->B being contraposed: it compiles to the edge
(A-B)[context] <- (A->B)[term:presumption], deriving not-B -> not-A from
the delegation. That is defeasible contraposition in action, and it is
what this task must generalise.

Shape of a transposed delegated edge (verified on a hand-built graph):
using A->B (presumption) to derive B from presumed A, with a strict
refutation of B, the transposal A[c] <- B[c], (A->B)[t] carries the
refutation to A. Result: A[t] UNDEC, A[c] UNDEC, (A->B)[t] IN, B[c] IN.
UNDEC rather than OUT is correct: A is itself presumed, so after the
transposal A is *contested* - its own presumption on the term side
against the delegated modus tollens on the context side - and grounded
declines to decide (the mode policy of Layer 4 then resolves it).
Compare the strict case: a declared A->B gives a strict transposal,
which bypasses the guard and sinks A[t] to OUT outright. That is the
whole effect of strictness under this design: it decides whether the
contraposed attack is decisive or merely contesting. Contrast the fused
edge of today, under which A simply survives as IN.

Where the strict rules went. A strict axiom used inside an argument does
not become a strict edge. =pArg=, which derives P from a presumed Q via
the declared axiom =pRule : Q->P=, compiles to one edge
=P[t] <- Q[t:presumption]=, marked *defeasible* because of its premise.
The rule and its premise are fused, and the edge's strictness is decided
by the premise. The rule's transposal =Q[c] <- P[c]= (modus tollens) is
therefore never generated.

Consequence, measured (hand-built graphs, same session):

:                        labels: P OUT, Q IN, P[c] IN

:                        labels: P OUT, Q OUT, P[c] IN, Q[c] IN

With P strictly refuted, Q should fall; today it stays IN. The correct

Found 2026-09-16 by the author, from an attack in
tests/minicourse/lesson5_labels.fspy that failed:
=attack d6 test d5= with =test= a counterargument to B->C, against =cArg=,
which derives C from A and B via the axiom abc : A->(B->C).

*Why the attack fails.* =cArg= never proves B->C. The two =elim= steps
build the elimination stack =?A * !B * r=, and B->C occurs only as the
type of that stack's tail - a *context*:

: A->B->C   context:Cons, term:DI
: B->C      context:Cons                 <- no term-side node
: C         context:ID,   term:Mu

A counterargument attacks term-side nodes, so =ThetaExpander= finds no
target and =Argument.attack= raises. Strictness and default status are
not the cause: an attack on the conclusion C (neither default nor
strict) succeeds. An argument *for* B->C does find the context node, but
the result is the proof fed into the stack as an alternative route; the
graph shows no conflict at all.

*The spec disagrees with this.* debate-graph-spec.org says theta-normal
form exposes one open obligation per logically progressing node. The
step that eliminates A is such a node, and its conclusion is B->C; the
implementation only exposes nodes that already exist in the term.

*Half 1 - theta-expansion (verified 2026-09-16).* Naming the partial
application turns the intermediate conclusion into a term:

: before :  < abc  ||  ?A * !B * r >
: after  :  < mu b:B->C.< abc || ?A * b >  ||  !B * r >

The rewritten term replays in Fellowship (instructions: cut (B -> C),
cut (A -> B -> C), axiom abc, elim, next, moxia b, elim, by default,
next, moxia r, ...), and once it is in this form the challenge to B->C
attacks it successfully.

*Half 2 - the compiler (verified 2026-09-16).* Naming is not enough.
=compile_debate= still emits a single edge for the whole argument,
C[t] <- A[t], B[t], so the intermediate conclusion is not a node on the
path to C. With A and B presumed and a strict refutation of B->C
attacking the rewritten term:

: edges  : B->C[c] <- (none, strict)      C[t] <- A[t], B[t]
: labels : B->C[c] IN,   C[t] IN

The attack lands and is accepted, yet C is untouched. The spec requires
the presentation to be "closed under subarguments (every subargument of a
registered argument contributes its own hyperedge)"; the compiler only
emits edges for whole arguments (the host and each scion). It must emit
C[t] <- B->C[t], B[t] and B->C[t] <- A[t].

*Why it is gnarly* (the author's word):

- A curried implication with n premises has n-1 intermediate
  conclusions. Expanding every one is linear and deterministic (one mu
  per elimination step), but =ThetaExpander= is driven by the issue
  proposition, so it has to find the right prefix of the spine - the one
  whose type is the issue - and split the stack exactly there.
- The same implication may be applied several times, and the same
  intermediate type may occur on several spines; which occurrences are
  targets is the same universal-substitution question as for leaf sites.
- The split interacts with =aida-strict-layer=: once the implication is
  an edge of its own, each elimination step becomes an implication edge
  with one premise, and each of those has a transposal. Designing the
  two together avoids doing the spine decomposition twice.
- The first-order analogue is the instantiation spine: a partially
  instantiated =forall x, A x -> B x= has intermediate conclusions too
  (=ConsFO= in the stack), so the same splitting is needed once
  =aida-first-order-reduction= opens that fragment.
- The compiler must recognise the named partial application as a
  subargument (a mu-bound term with a proposition, not a scaffold),
  which it currently has no shape for.

*The rule.* "Presumption" is the implementation's word; *delegation* is
the author's, and it is the accurate one. Writing =!1:P= does not just
assert P by default - it hands the burden of refuting P to the opposing
side. The skeptic now owes a context witness. So the onus always sits on
exactly one side of a proposition, and a presentation in which BOTH sides
delegate is ill-formed: each has passed the burden to the other and
neither can discharge it. Obligations are the dual and pose no problem -
nobody delegated, both sides simply stay OUT until an argument arrives -
and the normal pairing is a presumption on one side against an obligation
on the other, which is the same delegation seen from both ends.

*Why it is a separate task.* Enforcing it needs the onus to be *tracked*:
at registration time the wrapper (or the prover) must know, per
proposition, which side currently holds the burden, and reject a
`by default.` that would take it from a side that has already delegated.
That is a front-end change, independent of the acceptance-condition
compiler, and the author asked to keep the two apart.

Raised by the author 2026-09-25 while framing soundness and completeness
with respect to classical logic, and first filed under T1, which owned the
well-definedness of the strategy. Moved up on 2026-10-08 when T1 was
dropped; "target T1" below refers to debate-graph-spec.org.

*Why it is not free.* It does not follow from confluence. The calculus is
deliberately NOT confluent at the critical pair between a mu and a
mu-tilde, and resolving that pair by the labelling is the whole design
(Layer 4, call-by-onus.org). Different strategies therefore disagree in
general; the lemma says they cannot disagree about STRICTNESS.

*Proof sketch to try.* The strict phase runs before sigma and decides
every scaffold at which one wing is strict in the debate. So every
scaffold sigma still sees has both wings non-strict, i.e. both carry an
open site. At a support scaffold either choice keeps one wing, so a site
survives. At an attack, keeping the original keeps its sites, and defeat
builds the clash mu alpha.< t || e > from both wings, so a site survives
there too. Hence no residual choice creates or destroys strictness.

*The hazard.* Affine discard removes an undemanded site, and Layer 0
records that as an EQUATION, so a term carrying a site can reduce to one
without. "A site survives" therefore has to be stated up to discard, and
the induction has to track which sites are demanded. That is the same
side condition as T3's discard clause, so the two proofs share it.

*Two things to pin before the statement is well-posed.*

1. WHICH sigma. The implemented reduction is guided by a witness
   labelling, and credulous and skeptical resolve differently, so the
   lemma must fix the sigma the mode selects or quantify over the
   admissible ones.
2. What "strict term" means. Per the definition settled 2026-09-25
   (=aida-strict-phase-experimental=, written up in call-by-onus.org): no
   open site, and nothing else. Clash-freedom is a SEPARATE conjunct,
   needed for the strict EDGE and not for the term, so the lemma is about
   sites only.

*Where it sits in the larger claim.* This is the no-loss half of what the
author called soundness. The half that deserves that name runs the other
way - every statement the system labels IN through a strict edge is
classically derivable from the declarations - and is close to free
already: a strict edge is emitted only for a closed subterm, every free
name of it is declared, and the type oracle replays every unfolded term
through Fellowship, so the edge carries a typed closed proof term. Note
also that with inconsistent declarations everything is derivable, so the
clash exclusion is a RELEVANCE condition, not a soundness one. On the
other side, completeness via Peirce's law needs closure of the debate
layer under modus ponens and substitution, which is T1's grafting
stability, so Peirce is the critical witness rather than the proof.

*How to detect it.* The author's starting point is the prover's own message
for a classical proof. What was found in the source on 2026-10-01:

- The message is ="!!! The proof is classical; converted to the following
  LJ + EM proof"= (=wrap/fellowship/print.ml:161-168=). It is printed by
  =pretty_natural=, which runs only when a finished proof is exported in
  natural language (=instructions.ml:301-306=), and only under lk. It is not
  emitted during proof construction or at =qed=.
- The test behind it is =lk_to_lj_plus_em pt <> pt=, built on the
  =linear_term= / =linear_context= / =linear_command= functions in
  =interpreter_proof_terms.ml:267-307=. That is the linearity check the
  paper's definition asks for, already in the prover.
- So the wrapper cannot catch the message as things stand. Two routes:
  expose the check (for instance a field in the machine payload at =qed=, or
  a note on the tactic that makes the term non-linear), which needs an fsp
  rebuild; or run the same check in the wrapper on the parsed proof term.

*Alternative route, proposed by the author 2026-10-01: switch the calculus
per argument.* Enable logic switching in the prover and have the wrapper
select lj for each argument and lk for debates. Read in the source the same
day, not implemented or tested:

- Today the switch is refused after the first instructions ("Only the first
  instructions can be used to specify a logical setting"). The guard is the
  single =too_late= flag in =logic_switch= (=instructions.ml:111-116=); the
  calculus itself is the two global refs =lj= and =minimal=, which the
  tactics read at each step (12 sites in =tactics.ml=). Allowing the switch
  whenever the prover is =Idle=, and never inside an open proof, looks like
  a small change. =fsp= must be rebuilt.
- Gain over a linearity check: the refusal comes from the calculus, at the
  tactic that makes the step classical, and nothing depends on the
  =linear_*= functions agreeing with the paper. The "exactly once or at most
  once" question below is answered by lj itself.
- The export code assumes one logic per session: the Coq header is chosen
  from =!lj= when the file is started (=interpreter.ml:157-160=) and a
  finished proof is converted according to =!lj= at export time, not at
  proof time (=interpreter_proof_terms.ml:415=, =print.ml:162=). With
  switching, the logic would have to be recorded per theorem, or export
  restricted.
- =prop_of_csq= asserts on the shape of the conclusion list under lj
  (=core.ml:619-622=); that is safe only if a switch cannot happen while
  goals exist.
- A theorem proved under lk stays usable by =axiom= in a later lj argument.
  Whether an argument may cite a debate's result strictly is a design
  decision, not a prover matter.
- The wrapper replays arguments (the type oracle, scaffolds) and already
  tracks the session logic (=wrap/prover.py:613=, the "debates are
  classical" guard in =wrap/cli.py=); every replay path has to switch too.
- The refusal text is the prover's ("th is neither in your hypothesis nor in
  your conclusion"); the wrapper would have to say that the argument left
  the intuitionistic fragment.
- Counterarguments need a dual-intuitionistic mode (at most one hypothesis),
  which the prover does not have; it would be a third setting next to =lj=
  in the same tactic sites.

*Open points.*

- Whether the prover's =linear_*= functions agree with the paper's
  definition of a linearly bound variable (exactly one occurrence, no
  intervening binder of the same kind) has not been checked.
- The prover has no dual-intuitionistic mode and no dual-linearity check
  was found, so the counterargument half has no existing test to reuse.
- Affine binders (a conclusion never used) are ordinary in the fixtures;
  whether "linear" is meant strictly or as "at most once" must be settled
  against the paper before fixtures are refused.
- Fixtures that are classical as single arguments today have not been
  surveyed.
