What the table shows, in short:
- *RQ 4 and RQ 6 are expressible everywhere, at a price.* Every tool
  reaches AIDA's verdicts on the meta-norm and the burden of proof. But
  the asymmetry AIDA has built in, that a counterargument defeats a
  presumption, has to be supplied in each tool by its own means:
  - Carneades: weights (presumptions 0.5, other arguments 1.0)
  - ASPIC+: premise preferences
  - Arg2P: a declared burden of persuasion, or superiority
  - DDL: superiority between rules
  - PROLEG and s(CASP): where a requirement is placed (rule body or
    exception; negation as failure or classical negation)

  Without these, ASPIC+ (plain) and Arg2P leave 4c, 6b and 6c
  credulous or undecided.
- *Fragment (g) splits the tools into two groups.*
  - Arg2P (3 stable labellings, 2 pro and 1 con, as in AIDA) and
    s(CASP) keep both court positions.
  - Carneades, DDL (ambiguity blocking) and the PROLEG core decide con.
    The tie over constitutive effect and influence leaves both positions
    OUT, so the presumption at the top, the restraint, stands.
  - In those three tools the con outcome is a consequence of how they
    break ties, not of anything the courts argued. In AIDA the con
    outcome is one of three labellings, and fragment (g) shows where the
    courts part.
  - Plain ASPIC+ keeps both positions too, but with 5 stable extensions,
    since it builds the contrapositions of every strict rule as further
    arguments.
- *RQ 7, the cycle, separates the semantics most clearly.*
  - Two readings, as in AIDA: s(CASP) (stable models) and Arg2P (stable).
  - Undecided: Carneades (grounded-style), and grounded in Arg2P and
    AIDA.
  - The cycle accepted in one model: DDL accepts it in 7a, and in 7c it
    keeps the duty and the claim, supported only by each other, after
    the doubt has defeated the examination. That happens in both the
    ambiguity-blocking and the well-founded variant of the
    implementation. Whether this comes from the implementation or from
    encoding the duty as an obligation in a rule body was not checked.
  - Not terminating: the PROLEG core. Prolog's depth-first search does
    not end on the cycle, and 7b terminates only if the anchor clause
    comes first.
  - ASPIC+ with preferences closes the cycle in the court's favour. The
    preferences decide the case, so the two readings disappear.

* Per tool

** Carneades 4
- Mapping in =carneades/gen.py=:
  - a fact is an assumption
  - a presumption is an argument without premises, at weight 0.5
  - an owed premise is one nobody argues for
  - the helper is one linked argument over the requirements
- Carneades accepts assumptions outright. A rebuttable presumption has to
  be an argument. That was found by running 4c and 6c with assumptions,
  where the objection and the doubt had no effect.
- Ties within an issue leave both positions OUT. That decides fragment
  (g) for the restraint.
- The proof's win over the doubt (6d) can only be stated through
  weights: the doubt rests on a presumed rule, so it gets 0.5.
- The cycle stays undecided (7a, 7c). The doubt in 7c does not propagate
  through it.

** ASPIC+ (PyArg)
- Mapping in =pyarg/aspic.py=:
  - a fact is an axiom
  - a presumption is an ordinary premise
  - a presumed rule is defeasible
  - the helper is an ordinary premise with one strict rule per
    requirement
  - strict rules are closed under transposition, which is ASPIC+'s own
    condition for its rationality postulates
- PyArg's default ordering is last-link elitist with empty preferences,
  not "every attack defeats". Under it, a premise beats a
  defeasible-rule-based doubt (6c: =in=). The runner therefore reports
  three variants: plain, PyArg's default, and premise preferences
  (=output.txt=).
- ASPIC+ builds every argument the knowledge base allows. That includes
  reductio arguments no court stated. In 7a, the presumed propriety of
  the demand contraposes into "no limited scope, so no claim, so no
  duty". And the presumed passing of the examination makes the transfer
  agreement count as submitted. Hence 3 stable extensions where AIDA,
  which uses only the arguments the document states, has 2.
- PyArg's own stable routine needed 166 s for 7a (32 arguments, 93
  defeats). clingo with ASPARTIX's =stable.dl= computes the same
  extensions in milliseconds.

** Arg2P
- Mapping in =arg2p/run.py=:
  - presumptions are ordinary premises, or exceptions with weak negation
  - burdens are =bp/1=
- Profile =aspic= (no preference counts). Under =aspicPlus= the default
  last-link ordering decides 6c and 7c for the face-value premise, as in
  PyArg.
- The profile refuses a contrary to the conclusion of a strict rule. So
  rules with a contested conclusion had to be defeasible, including the
  proof in 6d, which then deadlocks with the doubt. A superiority under
  =aspicPlus= settles it (=rq6-d-sup=).
- The burden of persuasion is the closest construct to AIDA's burden
  allocation among the tools. =bp(correct)= turns 6c's deadlock into OUT,
  =bp(claim)= closes the cycle against the claim (7a), and
  =bp(registrable)= decides fragment (g) for the restraint. =bp(codet)=
  does not settle fragment (g); the reason was not investigated.
- On fragment (g), Arg2P finds the same three stable labellings as AIDA.

** s(CASP)
- Mapping in =scasp/gen.py=:
  - presumptions are defaults =p :- not -p=
  - counterarguments are rules for =-p=
  - the first-order, reified form is kept as in AIDA
- It agrees with AIDA's stable verdicts in every scenario, credulously
  and skeptically. That is expected: both compute founded readings, and
  AIDA's stable semantics is the founded reading the author chose.
- Model counts are not comparable, because s(CASP) returns partial models
  relevant to the query.
- What s(CASP) does not have: the argument as an object with its
  delegations and obligations, debates with moves, and the distinction
  between a presumed and an owed site within one argument. The burden
  lives in the choice between negation as failure and classical
  negation.

** Defeasible Deontic Logic (clingo implementation)
- Mapping in =ddl/metanorm.lp= and =ddl/frag_g.lp=:
  - the duty to enter is =obligation(enter)= from a prescriptive rule
  - presumptions are rules without body
  - requirements are rules for =non(passed)=, superior to the helper
- The deontic modality is native here, unlike in any other tool,
  including AIDA, where the duty is an ordinary predicate over a
  reified eventuality.
- Every conflict needs a stated superiority. Without one, ambiguity
  blocking leaves both sides unproved. That is why fragment (g) comes
  out con.
- The cycle: see Results. The duty and the claim survive the doubt in 7c.

** PROLEG core (re-implementation)
- =proleg/proleg.pl= follows PROLEG's published semantics (rule and
  exception). It is not the original system.
- The rule/exception split states the burden directly. RQ 4 and RQ 6
  read naturally.
- Fragment (g) is decided by where the exceptions sit. Köln's
  presumption is an exception to Oldenburg's rule, so the con outcome
  follows from the encoding.
- Cycles do not terminate. That is a limit of the depth-first proof
  procedure, and PROLEG states it as an assumption on rule bases.

AI NOTE on processing: AIDA and s(CASP) both compute the right order in
every scenario. What AIDA adds is the onus in the certificate. The entry
order's justification marks =!IN:MetaNorm= and =!IN:Passed e1= as
presumed, so the output says which of its grounds were never
established. s(CASP)'s justification tree records which literals hold
by negation as failure, which is the closest counterpart.

* Processing side by side: AIDA and Arg2P

The same six scenarios as AIDA's layers 2b and 2c
(=../rq4-6-7-metanorm.org=), in Arg2P with Prolog terms and the query
=lawful(O)= (=arg2p/processing.py=, output =arg2p/processing.txt=,
2026-11-15). Both compute the same order in every scenario:

| Scenario | AIDA: head of the normal form | AIDA: rests on (delegations) | Arg2P: =lawful(O)= accepted for | Arg2P: presumed premises; assumed not shown |
|----------+-------------------------------+------------------------------+---------------------------------+---------------------------------------------|
| 6p-a complete | =o_entry= | =!IN:MetaNorm=, =!IN:Passed e1= | =o_entry= | =f_meta=, =face_value=; - |
| 6p-b missing application | =o_submit= | =!IN:Lawful o_submit= | =o_submit= | -; =duty=, =submitted(d_appl)= |
| 6p-c register conflict | =o_prove= | =!IN:Conflict kp_c->Lawful o_prove= | =o_prove= (needs =bp(correct(kp_c))=, bpGrounded) | -; =duty= |
| 6p-d doubt cleared | =o_entry= | as 6p-a | =o_entry=, by two arguments (needs =sup(evidence, doubt)=) | =f_meta=, =face_value= / =f_meta=; - |
| 6w-a submitted | open | the finding is defeated | nothing | |
| 6w-b missing, computed | =interim d_appl= | =!IN:Missing d_appl= | =interim(d_appl)= | =finding=; - |

What is the same:
- *Computed objects.* Both return a term, not only a yes or no.
  =interim(d_appl)= is computed by unification in Arg2P and by cut
  elimination in AIDA (an existential's witness meeting its
  elimination). Both start from a generic rule that never names the
  document.
- *Presumptions in the output.* Both say what the result rests on
  without having established it.
  - Arg2P: the argument's ordinary premises and the weak-negation
    conditions of its rules ("the duty is not shown").
  - AIDA: the =!IN= sites of the normal form.
  - AIDA's presumed "not owed" site of layer 2c and Arg2P's =~(duty)= are
    the same idea.

Where they differ:
- *One witness or all instances.* Arg2P answers a query with a variable
  and lists every accepted instance, each with all its arguments: in
  6p-d two arguments support =lawful(o_entry)=. AIDA's argument concludes
  "some order is lawful". Its evaluation yields one normal form, one
  witness with one justification, and when two candidates stand it keeps
  one by its rule (=aida-witness-candidate-order= on main).
- *A term or a set.* AIDA's output is a cut-free proof term: the witness
  at its head and the justification as one composed argument, which the
  renderer prints as one text. Arg2P's output is a labelling with
  argument records (identifier, rules, subarguments, attacks).
- *Classical existence.* AIDA's =decision_k= is one classical argument
  (an argued Peirce step) that proves an order exists without deciding
  the duty; the evaluation resolves the case split. Arg2P has no
  classical reasoning inside arguments. The case split lives in the rule
  base as two rules guarded by =~(duty)=, so the "argument for existence"
  is not one object.
- *The burden.*
  - AIDA's onus is part of every site, so no scenario needed anything
    extra.
  - Arg2P needed a burden of persuasion for the doubt (6p-c) and a
    superiority for the proof (6p-d), both as found in =run.py=. Without
    them, 6p-c and 6p-d deadlock under the =aspic= profile.
- *The encodings differ in one place that shows in the output.* Arg2P's
  examination is a closed conjunction, so the face-value presumption is a
  premise of the entry order's argument and appears in its record. In
  AIDA the examination is the helper =Passed=, presumed, with one denial
  per requirement. The face-value presumption acts in the labelling (it
  defeats the correctness objection) and does not appear in the normal
  form.
- *Size.* The Arg2P theories are 10 to 16 lines each. AIDA's blocks are
  proof scripts several times longer.

** Reading the comparison

AI NOTE (observations for the authors):
- *Where Arg2P is ahead.* It is ahead as a tool and in breadth: more
  semantics, preferences and burdens of persuasion that can themselves
  be argued, meta-argumentation about rules, deontic and temporal
  vocabulary, causality tests, ABA and abstract inputs, scale, packaging,
  editors, and AI assistants. For the IRIS question of open texture (RQ
  8), =metaRules= states directly which test applies to the need: the
  readings become arguments (=features.txt=, check 4).
- *Where AIDA is distinct.*
  - It is closer to a proof assistant for arguments than to a rule-based
    reasoner. Arguments are authored interactively, type-checked, named
    and cited, while Arg2P generates its arguments from rules and
    returns them as records.
  - It treats a debate as a sequence of moves.
  - It puts the burden of proof into every site, with a consistency
    check.
  - It evaluates a debate to a program-like normal form, with a witness
    and a certificate.
  - It reasons classically inside arguments, including existence proofs
    that do not decide the case.
  - It has quantifiers, existential statements and discourse referents.
- *Where they meet.* Both compute the same outputs on the IRIS
  processing examples (=processing.txt=). Both expose what an answer
  presumes. Both admit cycles and give two stable readings of the RQ 7
  circle.
- *Where AIDA lags as a tool.* Rendering, performance, packaging and
  documentation for users. The comparison suggests that AIDA's
  contribution is its calculus (onus per site, evaluation to normal
  forms, classical first-order debates), not its tooling.
- *Not compared.* Arg2P's explanation pages and its distributed solvers
  were not run. Its experimental modules (constraints, time) were read,
  not tried.

* Strengths and weaknesses of each system

** AIDA
Strengths:
- Debates as first-class objects. Order, replies and the course move by
  move are part of the model and can be queried.
- The burden of proof per site (delegation or obligation), with AIDA's
  own consistency check (no opposing presumptions). Moving a burden is
  one tactic.
- Processing. Evaluation returns a value, an exception, or an open term
  that names what is missing. Witness scaffolds return a concrete object
  with a certificate.
- Classical first-order logic with reified eventualities and discourse
  referents. Correlativity, quantified rules and reductio arguments are
  written as the law states them.
- Several semantics side by side (grounded, stable, skeptical,
  credulous). Cycles are represented, not refused. The founded reading
  is available.
- Literate formalisation with decorations next to the source text.

Weaknesses:
- Verbose. Arguments are proof scripts: 162 lines for scenario 7c
  against 16-37 elsewhere.
- Performance. The type-check replay is quadratic, so long debates time
  out on the stock build (the case-law debate's last stage); see the
  report on main.
- Rendering not ready for legal readers: language mixing, internal
  names. The tree renderer is deprecated.
- The labelling is not yet closed under transposition. A labelling can
  accept a rule and its antecedent while rejecting its consequent (see
  Classicality).
- No derived conjunction, and no preference across the support and
  attack stacks.
- Features spread over branches: =extends= and registered debates are on
  main, discourse referents on this branch.
- Bugs met on the way: binder capture, subtraction introduction, a
  reserved word.
- Needs knowledge of the calculus to write arguments.

** Carneades 4
Strengths:
- Designed for legal argument and the burden of proof: proof standards,
  critical questions as scheme exceptions and assumptions.
- Weighing functions (cumulative, criteria, preferences).
- Compact YAML. Argument generation from schemes. Cycles in version 4.
- Used by the DIREGA group already.

Weaknesses:
- A tie within an issue leaves both positions OUT, which decided
  fragment (g) for the restraint.
- Assumptions are accepted outright, not presumed. Presumptions need
  premise-less arguments and weights.
- One labelling only: alternative readings (RQ 7, fragment (g)) are not
  shown.
- No move order and no output object.
- Numeric weights where AIDA uses the onus.
- Ground statements; no quantified rules.

** ASPIC+ (PyArg)
Strengths:
- A standard structured framework with a large literature.
- Arguments are generated exhaustively, including ones no participant
  stated (the reductio readings of 7a). That is a check for hidden
  consequences.
- Preferences and orderings are explicit and configurable. PyArg is easy
  to install and has explanation algorithms.

Weaknesses:
- Without preferences, a presumption and its counterargument attack
  each other symmetrically. Every burden has to be supplied as a
  preference.
- The default last-link ordering changed 6c's verdict.
- Exhaustive generation also produces arguments no court made (3 to 5
  extensions where AIDA has 2 or 3).
- Ground and propositional here. PyArg's own stable routine needed 166 s
  for 32 arguments.
- No debate, no output object.

** Arg2P
Strengths:
- The burden of persuasion as a construct (=bp/1=, two-stage labelling).
  It is the closest to AIDA's onus among the tools, and it can itself be
  argued (=graphExtension(bp)=).
- Meta-argumentation:
  - rules apply only if argued applicable (=metaRules=); on the IRIS
    threshold question this gives one labelling per reading
  - conflicts and preferences can be concluded by rules
- Breadth:
  - 17 semantics
  - strict rules closed under transposition automatically
  - obligations and permissions
  - causality tests (NESS, but-for)
  - event calculus and constraints (experimental)
  - ABA and abstract frameworks
  - Prolog computation inside rules
- Tooling: the same API on the JVM, JavaScript and Python; a web
  playground; a VS Code extension with a language server; an MCP server
  and skills for AI assistants; compact graphs and parallel solving for
  large theories; near-daily releases.
- On the IRIS material:
  - the same three stable labellings as AIDA on fragment (g)
  - the same orders, =interim(d_appl)= included, as query bindings

Weaknesses:
- Profiles refuse a contrary to a strict conclusion, so a proof that
  should be strict had to be defeasible (6d deadlocked without a
  superiority).
- Burdens are declared per statement. A burden on =codet= did not
  settle fragment (g); one on =registrable= did.
- No debates as sequences of moves. No classical reasoning inside an
  argument. No existential statements. No generated natural-language
  text.
- The default last-link ordering decided 6c and 7c for the face-value
  premise, until the =aspic= profile was chosen.
- Installation needs Python ≤ 3.12 for the Python package.

** s(CASP)
Strengths:
- First-order without grounding.
- Its stable-model semantics agreed with AIDA's stable verdicts in every
  scenario.
- Justification trees, constraints and arithmetic (counting; the DIREGA
  notes value this).
- Computes output objects as bindings (the lawful order).

Weaknesses:
- No argument objects and no debates. The burden is only the choice
  between negation as failure and classical negation.
- Answer counts are partial models, so they are not comparable.
- The DIREGA notes report unusable Prolog features (=forall/2=) and
  suspected bugs.
- Named anonymous variables are refused.

** Defeasible Deontic Logic (clingo implementation)
Strengths:
- Native deontic modalities: obligation, permission, compensation
  (contrary-to-duty).
- Superiority as an explicit priority. A well-understood proof theory,
  efficient in principle.

Weaknesses:
- Every conflict needs a stated superiority, otherwise ambiguity
  blocking leaves both sides unproved (fragment (g): con).
- In this implementation the RQ 7 cycle is accepted as an unfounded loop
  (7c keeps duty and claim after the doubt). Whether that is the
  implementation or the encoding was not checked.
- One module has unsafe rules. Ground atoms. No explanation and no
  debate.

** PROLEG (core re-implemented)
Strengths:
- The rule/exception split is the burden of proof of a legal doctrine.
  Lawyers read it directly.
- Small and transparent.

Weaknesses:
- The original is not public.
- Depth-first evaluation does not terminate on cycles, and it depends on
  clause order (7b).
- One answer: alternatives are invisible. Fragment (g) is decided by
  where the exceptions sit.

* Classicality: every implication contraposes

AIDA reasons inside an argument in classical first-order sequent
calculus. Every implication, a presumed one included, can be used
backwards (modus tollens), and a debate's scaffolds are classical: they
"throw to a second conclusion, which LJ forbids" (=README.md=). The other
six systems use directional rules for what is defeasible. ASPIC+ requires
its strict rules to be closed under transposition, and Arg2P closes them
automatically (=autoTransposition=, checked in =arg2p/features.txt=). Both
keep defeasible rules directional.

AI NOTE (correction, 2026-11-15): an earlier version of this file said
Arg2P does not close strict rules automatically. That was wrong.
Arg2P's run of 2026-11-14 used the =aspic= profile, under which
transposition is on as well.

What it buys, in this material:
- *Reductio as the courts use it.* Oldenburg refutes the principle "late
  filing defeats the interest in registration" by modus tollens. If it
  held, facts entered only declaratorily could not be registrable, but
  § 15 (2) HGB presupposes that they are (fragment (c)).
  - In AIDA this is one argument over the principle's instance.
  - In a directional system the backward rule has to be written as a rule
    of its own, which no longer shows that it is the same principle.
- *One rule, both directions.* The register court's principle "the
  register shows liability only" serves as "no liability, so no need" and
  as "a need, so liability" (=p_liab_only= is stated in the second form,
  =vocabulary.org=). Correlativity of claim and duty is two universal
  rules, and either can be used in either direction.
- *Classical first-order debates.* Witnesses, reductio over quantifiers,
  the drinker paradox: AIDA's tests show debates that directional
  systems cannot state without a classical principle as a premise.
- *Disagreement located where it is.* With contraposition, Köln's
  presumption that the usufructuary has no influence, together with
  Oldenburg's presumed rule "§ 1071 ⇒ co-determination" and the strict
  § 1071, is inconsistent. One of the two presumptions must go. That is
  what Köln's reasons actually dispute: whether a consent right gives
  influence.

What it costs, or risks:
- *Contrapositives nobody argued.* A presumed rule used backwards can
  produce arguments a court would not make. Two examples from the
  material:
  - "A register conflict shows the report incorrect" contraposes to
    "the report is presumed correct, so the register does not
    contradict it". That is harmless only because the conflict is a
    strict fact here.
  - The BGH rule "a claim limits the scope" contraposes to "the scope is
    not limited, so there is no claim". That is the second reading of the
    RQ 7 circle, which the IRIS draft criticises as circular.

  ASPIC+ shows what full generation of such arguments does (3-5
  extensions where AIDA has 2-3). AIDA considers only the arguments the
  document states, so the author controls which contrapositions enter.
- *Not yet closed in the labelling.* AIDA's labelling is not closed under
  transposition (task =aida-strict-layer= on main: no premise attacks
  through strict edges).
  - In fragment (g)'s con labelling the accepted statements are § 1071
    (strict), Oldenburg's presumed rule (IN) and the refutation of
    co-determination (IN). They are classically inconsistent.
  - With the closure, Köln's counterargument would also defeat Oldenburg's
    rule, and the con reading would state that rejection explicitly.
  - Until then, classicality holds inside arguments but not across the
    labelling.
- *Spelling.* =~A= and =A -> false= are the same statement classically
  but were spelled differently in the RQ 3 files. That forced the
  type-check replay of the whole expanded term (62k nodes) until one
  spelling was chosen (task =aida-negation-spelling-in-unfolding= on
  main).
- *Inconsistency.* Contradicting strict facts do not make everything
  follow. A clash surfaces as an =EXCEPTION= (=README.md=). This was not
  exercised in this material.

AI NOTE: the comparison is about this material. AIDA's design decision to
contrapose defeasible implications is AIDA's own and is not judged here
by another framework's conventions. The examples show where it helps
the legal reading and where the author has to decide which contrapositive
to state.

* Candidate claims for the paper

AI NOTE: for the authors to adopt or reject.
- Every compared system can reach the verdicts of RQ 4 and RQ 6 on this
  material. They differ in where the burden of proof is stated:
  - in AIDA, as the polarity of single sites in an argument (presumed or
    owed)
  - elsewhere, as weights, preferences, superiority, burden declarations
    or rule placement
- On the core of the RQ 3 dispute, three of the six other systems decide
  the case by their tie-breaking. AIDA, Arg2P and s(CASP) keep both court
  positions, which is what IRIS RQ 3 asks to model.
- The circular justification of RQ 7 is represented without refusal by
  AIDA, s(CASP) and Arg2P, as two stable readings. It is accepted
  silently by the DDL implementation, and it does not terminate in
  PROLEG-style evaluation.
- AIDA models a decision as a course of moves, not only as an outcome.
  Oldenburg's decision is a sequence of objections and answers that
  alternate the issue's status, and München's decision turns only when
  the BGH counter-instances defeat the "interest in information"
  generalisation.
- A gap in codified law can be modelled as a process: an append-only
  record whose status changes. Open enumeration is one more supporting
  argument per example, and open structure is one more denial per
  necessary condition. What remains open at the end is exactly what the
  latest decisions dispute.
- Once a debate is complete, its result can be executed. AIDA's
  evaluation returns the order the court has to make, with a certificate
  that marks the grounds it presumed rather than established.
