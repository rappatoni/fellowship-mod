* About this sequel

minicourse.org ends its account of evaluation at lesson 7: sigma decides
each scaffold, then the term is normalised. This sequel takes that
sentence apart. Lesson 8a first builds the debate term from the
document: the unfolding that minicourse.org only mentions. Lessons 9-13
then start where unfolding and labelling have finished - with a debate
term and a witness labelling sigma in hand - and follow the term through
every phase to its normal form, one reduction at a time.

Prerequisites: lesson 2 (hyperedges), lesson 6 (a scaffold is a critical
pair) and lesson 7 (how sigma is chosen, derivation status, the wing
table).

The code under study is =_evaluate_under= in =core/comp/evaluate.py=:

:   term, sigma
:     |  phase 1  strict_resolve     core/dc/strict.py        decide by strictness
:     |  phase 2  resolve_scaffolds  core/comp/evaluate.py    decide by sigma
:     |  phase 3  normalize_strong   core/comp/oracle_terms.py  base strategy
:     v  classify_nf                 core/comp/oracle_terms.py  value / exception / open

*Two routes.* What this sequel describes is the pipeline on the *unfolded*
term. It is the CLI default (=pipeline unfolded.=), since 2026-10-06 again:
only this route builds the stacked shape and the argument entrypoints of
lesson 8a. The other route, =pipeline shared.=, keeps the debate as named
sub-debates and runs phases 1-3 one sub-debate at a time; it still builds
the older, legacy shape (tasks.org, aida-shared-route-stack-shape) and is
the subject of minicourse-sharing.org (lessons 14-18).

*Is phase 2 normalisation?* Yes. Every decision phases 1 and 2 take is
the choice of one rule, (mu<) or (>mu), at one critical pair - the
scaffold's inner pair. What makes them look like something else is that
the implementation does not fire that rule as a reduction step. It
recognises a scaffold by its shape and replaces it in one rewrite by the
wing it keeps: the decided step, plus the bookkeeping step before it and
an eta step after it. Lessons 10 and 11 replay those rewrites as the
individual steps they abbreviate, and lesson 13 explains why the
implementation cannot currently take them one at a time.

* Lesson 8a - Unfolding: from the document to a debate term

(Numbered 8a so that lessons 9-18, and the files and tests named after
them, keep their numbers.)

** Definition

*The input: the document graph.* Every atomic argument is compiled when
it is registered (=document_add=, =wrap/prover.py=), with the compiler of
lesson 2, and its edges are merged into one graph, the document graph
(=prover.document=; =graph document.= prints it). There is one difference
from lesson 2: each edge also keeps the *body* it was compiled from
(=Edge.term=), with the sites still numbered as Fellowship numbered them
(=!1.2.1:A=). An edge says which statement an argument derives and from
which statements. Its body says how. Unfolding needs both.

The default markers of the graph (lesson 2) record, for each statement,
whether some edge of the document uses it as an obligation or as a
presumption.

*The output: a debate term, from an entrypoint* (=core/dc/unfold.py=).
There are two kinds of entrypoint, and they give two kinds of term:

  - an *issue*, a statement: =unfold_issue(graph, S)= gives the
    *canonical* term of the debate about S. The CLI writes an issue
    =issue :X= (X proved, the term side) or =issue X:= (X refuted);
  - an *argument*: =unfold_argument(graph, edge)= gives the term *biased
    towards* that argument, the one =evaluate pro.= and =explain pro.=
    work on. It answers "can this argument be defended?" rather than "is
    S acceptable?".

The two differ only in the order of one stack and in the kinds of the
sites in the argument's own body (below). The labelling plays no part
in unfolding: the term is built from the document alone, and labelled
afterwards.

*The shape.* The debate about a statement S, whose contrary is S̄:

: ATT( SUP( ROOT_S , STACK_S ) ,  SUP~( ROOT_S̄ , STACK_S̄ ) )
: STACK = SUP( SUP( P1, P2 ), P3 )        for supporters P1, P2, P3 in registration order

  - ROOT_S is S's own site: a presumption (=!u=, =u!=) if some argument
    of the document presumes S, an obligation (=?u=, =u?=) otherwise.
  - STACK_S holds the arguments deriving S as one stack of support
    scaffolds, the last registered outermost. Sigma reads it top-down,
    so the last registered is *judged first* (lesson 11), and the
    strict phase checks a supporter before what it supports (lesson
    10), so the order holds there too.
  - One support scaffold puts ROOT_S on top: =SUP(ROOT_S, STACK_S)=. The
    root is what is left if no supporter is accepted.
  - One attack scaffold wraps all of it. Its scion, the *attacker wing*,
    is the debate about the contrary *without its own attackers* (those
    are S's supporters): S̄'s root under S̄'s stack. The contrary's root
    stands for "somebody presumes or demands S̄ outright"; it is there
    whenever S has attackers or S̄ a default marker. It is always
    captured, by the attack's own =alt=, which binds S̄ (below): if no
    attacker succeeds, the proof of S is passed on as if unattacked.
  - Empty parts are left out: no supporters, no support scaffold; no
    attackers and no marker on S̄, no attack scaffold.

The scaffolds are the paper's shapes of lesson 6. On the term side, with
T for the supported or attacked term:

: SUP(T, S)   mu alt.< T || mu'b.< mu _.<b || alt> || mu'_.< S || alt > > >
: ATT(T, E)   mu alt.< T || mu'b.< mu _.<b || E >   || mu'_.< b || alt > > >

=SUP~= and =ATT~= are the context-side mirror images.

*An argument entrypoint* changes two things, and only for the argument
itself:

  - the argument goes on *top* of its statement's stack, so it is judged
    first: =SUP( ROOT_S, SUP( SUP(P2, P3), P1 ) )= for P1. A
    counterargument is a supporter of its own, context-side statement;
  - the sites directly in its *own body* keep the kind it wrote. Where it
    demands a statement that somebody else presumes, the root of that
    sub-debate is its own obligation and the presumption becomes the
    bottom of that statement's stack.

Below that, every sub-debate is canonical.

*The algorithm* is two mutually recursive functions. =statement(S, env,
spine)= gives the term (or context) standing for the statement S at the
current place. =env= maps statements to the binders in scope there, and
=spine= is the set of statements being expanded on the path from the
root. Two cases, tried in this order:

| case     | condition                 | result                         |
|----------+---------------------------+--------------------------------|
| captured | S is bound in =env=       | the binder's variable          |
| expanded | otherwise                 | the debate about S (=_debate=) |

=_debate= draws the root, builds the stack (=_stack=), the support
scaffold (=_supported=) and the attacker wing (=_wing=), in that order.
While a stack element is unfolded S is on the spine, and while the
attacker wing is unfolded its contrary is too. A statement on the spine
is always bound in =env= as well (below), so it is always captured. Should
one ever be on the spine without a binder, =statement= logs a warning
("no binder in scope") and leaves a bare site; on the fixtures this
never happens (=tests/test_scaffold_capture.py=).

Only argument edges become stack elements. A lambda's own subargument
edge (=SelfAttack.λ1= below) is a piece of its parent's body, and
appears where the parent is unfolded.

*A stack element* is an edge's body unfolded in place (=_edge=). The body
is copied, and while it is copied:

  - every site in it (Goal, Laog, Deleg, Geled) is replaced by
    =statement= for that site's statement: whatever the body had there,
    the debate about that statement takes its place. A =cite NAME= leaf
    is handled the same way, as a site for the cited argument's
    statement;
  - every binder puts its statement into =env= for its own body: a
    lambda's hypothesis =h:P= and a =mu'x:P= bind P[t] (a proof of P is
    in scope), a =mu a:P= and a lambda-bar bind P[c] (a refutation of P
    is). =env= is passed down into the elements unfolded inside the
    body, so the binders of every *enclosing* argument are in scope as
    well;
  - every binder is named by =fresh=: the body's own name the first time
    it occurs anywhere in the term, suffixed after that (=th_2=), because
    Fellowship's replay refuses a hypothesis introduced twice. For an
    argument entrypoint its own body is unfolded first, so it keeps its
    names.

*Every binder captures*, the scaffolds' own included (decided
2026-10-06, tasks.org aida-unfold-scaffold-binders-capture). On either
side a scaffold's =alt= binds the *contrary* of its statement and its =b=
the *statement itself*; the scion is in the scope of both, the supported
or attacked term in that of =alt= only. So, inside the debate about S:

  - a supporter of S that needs S gets the =b= of its scaffold. After the
    scaffold's outer step =b= is the supported term - the root, or the
    lower part of the stack - so the supporter *shares* it instead of
    starting a second obligation for S;
  - a supporter of S that needs S̄ gets the scaffold's =alt=;
  - the attacker wing's root, S̄, is the attack's =alt=; an attacker that
    needs S̄ gets the =b= of the wing's own support scaffold (that is, the
    root again), and one that needs S the wing's =alt=.

Every statement being expanded therefore has a binder in scope below its
debate, and capture alone ends the unfolding. The placeholders =_= bind
nothing.

*Numbers.* Sites, =alt= binders and =b= binders each have a counter of
their own (=_wiring= skips any name already used). A site is numbered
when its statement is expanded; an =alt= and its =b= when their scaffold
is begun, before its scion is built. So both grow from the root inwards,
and a captured root takes no number.

*A bare issue.* If nobody derives or contests the issue, the result is a
bare site, and =unfold= gives it the eta wrapper =mu x.< site || x >=
every argument has, so that the pipeline can read the issue off the root.

*Why capture* (the author's reading, 2026-10-05). Read the self-attack
below computationally. The opponent's default "P fails" is a
continuation for P that nobody has supplied: a bluff. Inside the lambda
the hypothesis =h:P= is a witness for P, and the body passes it to that
default continuation, =< h || P fails >=. So the opponent has produced,
or cooperated in producing, a witness for P. The proponent's binder
=mu rule:P= is a real continuation for P, and it has been waiting for
exactly such a witness. Capture replaces the default by the bound
continuation, =< h || rule >=, and the witness is thrown back to the
proponent. Two continuations for P compete, one of them a default, and
the default makes way for the real one. The term side is the mirror
image: a demand for P under a hypothesis =h:P= is met by =h=.

Capture does not depend on the kind of the site. An obligation and a
presumption are both holes and differ only in onus, who owes the
witness. =statement= tests =env= before it looks at anything else, and
the contrary's root is captured in the same way (=_bare=).

The variable that replaces the site no longer shows which onus the hole
had. A captured presumption is therefore marked (=captured_presumption=).
On this route the issue graph is compiled again from the unfolded term
(=compile_issue=), and the compiler records a captured variable as a
source of the binder's statement, a presumption source if it is marked
and an obligation source otherwise. The document graph already holds
that source; the mark is only needed for the round trip from the term
back to a graph. Whether the issue graph should be taken from the
document graph instead, and how a captured site that stays a source
fits with the strict phase, which does not count it as an assumption
(lesson 10), are open questions in tasks.org. Lesson 13 follows the
consequences.

*Termination and size.* A statement is expanded at most once on any path
(the spine): below its debate it is bound, so every later demand for it
is captured. A document has finitely many statements, so the term has
finite depth. But a statement reached along two paths is expanded on
both, so the term grows with the number of paths through the document
graph - exponentially in the worst case (minicourse-sharing.org, lesson
14).

*Terms are kept.* The term unfolded for an argument is kept on it
(=Argument.unfolded_body=), the canonical term of an issue on the
document, each with the document's *revision*. Registering an argument
or adding a declaration makes them stale - a new argument can support or
attack, a new declaration can make a presumption strict or refuted - and
the next use unfolds again. =unfold argument NAME.= and =unfold issue :X.=
build and print them; =render NAME [style] unfolded.= renders one.

*After unfolding.* The term is replayed through Fellowship (the type
check), compiled into the issue graph and labelled:

:   document graph, entrypoint
:     |  unfold          core/dc/unfold.py          this lesson
:     |  type check      core/dc/typecheck.py       Fellowship replays the term
:     |  compile_issue   core/dc/strict.py          lesson 2, plus strict edges (lesson 10)
:     |  labellings      core/comp/adf_label.py     lessons 3-5
:     v  sigma           core/comp/evaluate.py      lesson 7 (witness_labelling)

The issue graph and its labels are the same from every entrypoint; only
the term, and so the normal form, depends on it. Lesson 9 starts from
the end of that pipeline.

** Example

*The running example.* =graph document.= on
=tests/minicourse/lesson9_evaluation.fspy=:

: Document graph: 5 nodes, 4 edges (every registered atomic argument)
:   pro (argument, defeasible): B[t] <- A[t:pres]
:   con (argument, defeasible): A[c] <- C[t:pres]
:   killC (argument, strict): C[c] <- -
:   attackrule (argument, defeasible): A->B[c] <- D[t:pres]
:   default A[t]: presumption
:   default C[t]: presumption
:   default D[t]: presumption

and the bodies the first three edges keep:

: pro    μpro:B.<μth:B.<r1:A->B||!1.2.1:A*th:B>||pro:B>
: con    μ'con:A.<con:A||μ'x:A.<μth:~A.<r2:C->~A||!1.1.2.1:C*th:~A>||μ'H1:~A.<H1:~A||x:A*_F_>>>
: killC  μ'killC:C.<killC:C||μ'x:C.<nc:~C||μ'H1:~C.<H1:~C||x:C*_F_>>>

*What attackrule shows: only sites can be attacked.* =attackrule= refutes
=A -> B= from a presumption of D. It is meant to attack the rule pro
applies, =r1: A -> B=, but it takes no part in pro's debate: the term
below has no trace of it. The reason is that =r1= is a declared axiom.
In pro's body it is a leaf, a strict name, not a site; pro's edge is
=B[t] <- A[t]= and has no source A->B[t]; so nothing the unfolder walks
stands for the statement "A -> B is proved", and the refutation of that
statement is never reached. The document graph does have the node A->B,
but only its context side. In the current implementation the only
points at which an argument can be attacked (or supported) are its sites
and its conclusion: not the declared axioms it uses, not the lambda
subarguments inside it, not the intermediate conclusions of a curried
rule. To make r1 attackable today, pro has to take the rule as a premise
(=cut (A -> B) th. by default.=), which gives the site =!:A->B=. Whether
a declared axiom should be attackable at all - in ASPIC+ terms a strict
premise cannot be attacked, but it can rebut, which would make
attackrule OUT - is recorded in tasks.org
(aida-discuss-non-site-attack-points, with aida-strict-layer and
aida-curried-intermediate-conclusions on the graph side). Nothing reports
that attackrule is unreachable; it is silently left out.

=explain pro.= prints the unfolder's steps:

: unfold         B[t] expanded, root u1 (Goal), 1 supporter(s), 'pro' on top
: unfold           B[t] +support 'pro' (the argument unfolded, on top)
: unfold         A[t] expanded, root u2 (Deleg), 0 supporter(s)
: unfold           A[t] attacked by the debate about A[c], 1 supporter(s) (alt alt2, b b2)
: unfold         the bare A[c] captured as 'alt2'
: unfold         A[c], the attacker wing, root 'alt2'
: unfold           A[c] +support 'con'
: unfold         C[t] expanded, root u3 (Deleg), 0 supporter(s)
: unfold           C[t] attacked by the debate about C[c], 1 supporter(s) (alt alt4, b b4)
: unfold         the bare C[c] captured as 'alt4'
: unfold         C[c], the attacker wing, root 'alt4'
: unfold           C[c] +support 'killC'

Step by step:

1. *B[t]*, =env= and spine empty. Nobody presumes B, so the root is the
   obligation ?u1. One argument derives B, pro, which is also the
   entrypoint; its stack is pro alone, under the support scaffold =alt1=,
   =b1=, so =env= = {B[c]: alt1, B[t]: b1} inside it. Nobody derives
   B[c], and B[c] has no marker: no attack scaffold.
2. pro's body. Its binders =pro= and =th= are both continuations for B,
   so they shadow =alt1=: =env= = {B[c]: th, B[t]: b1}. At the site
   =!1.2.1:A= the unfolder asks for *A[t]*: not in =env=, so expanded. pro
   presumes A, and pro is the entrypoint, so the root is its own kind,
   the presumption !u2. Nobody derives A, so no support scaffold. con
   derives A[c], so A[t] is attacked: =alt2= (binds A[c]), =b2= (binds
   A[t]).
3. The attacker wing, the debate about *A[c]* without its attackers. Its
   root is A[c], and A[c] is bound to =alt2=: the root is =alt2=. Its
   stack is con, under the wing's support scaffold =alt3=, =b3=.
4. con's body. =con= and =x= bind A[t], =th= binds ~A[c] and is renamed
   =th_2=, since pro's =th= came first. At the site =!1.1.2.1:C= the
   unfolder asks for *C[t]*. With a TRACE log the state there is

   : unfold: at C[t]  env={B[c]:th, B[t]:b1, A[c]:b3, A[t]:x, B(S:A,->,false)[c]:th_2}  spine={A[t], A[c], B[t]}

   (=B(S:A,->,false)= is the key of ~A, which is not a node of the
   document, so the log prints the raw key). =env= holds the binders of
   every enclosing argument and scaffold; the innermost wins (=x= over
   =alt3= for A[t]). C[t] is not in it, so it is expanded: the
   presumption !u3, attacked by the debate about C[c] (=alt4=, =b4=) -
   its root captured as =alt4=, its stack killC (=alt5=, =b5=).
5. killC's body has no site, so the recursion stops. Its binders are
   renamed where taken: =x= becomes =x_2=, and its =H1= is the first
   =H1= in the term, so it is con's =H1=, met later, that becomes =H1_2=.

The result is the term lesson 9 starts from:

: μalt1:B.<?u1:B||μ'b1:B.<μ_:B.<b1:B||alt1:B>||μ'_:B.<μpro:B.<μth:B.<r1:A->B||μalt2:A.<!u2:A||μ'b2:A.<μ_:A.<b2:A||μ'alt3:A.<μb3:A.<μ_:A.<alt3:A||μ'con:A.<con:A||μ'x:A.<μth_2:~A.<r2:C->~A||μalt4:C.<!u3:C||μ'b4:C.<μ_:C.<b4:C||μ'alt5:C.<μb5:C.<μ_:C.<alt5:C||μ'killC:C.<killC:C||μ'x_2:C.<nc:~C||μ'H1:~C.<H1:~C||x_2:C*_F_>>>>||μ'_:C.<alt5:C||b5:C>>||alt4:C>>||μ'_:C.<b4:C||alt4:C>>>*th_2:~A>||μ'H1_2:~A.<H1_2:~A||x:A*_F_>>>>||μ'_:A.<alt3:A||b3:A>>||alt2:A>>||μ'_:A.<b2:A||alt2:A>>>*th:B>||pro:B>||alt1:B>>>

Here pro is B's only supporter, so its term is also the canonical term
of B: =unfold issue :B.= prints the same.

*Two entrypoints that differ.* =tests/entrypoints.fspy= has three
arguments for B, =p1=, =p2= and =p3=, each from a presumption of its own,
registered in that order. The canonical term of B and the term for p1
differ only in the stack:

: issue :B    SUP( ?u1:B, SUP( SUP(P1, P2), P3 ) )
: p1          SUP( ?u1:B, SUP( SUP(P2, P3), P1 ) )

All three are IN, so =evaluate issue :B.= returns p3's proof, the top of
the canonical stack, and =evaluate p1.= returns p1's own (lesson 11).

*A capture by b.* =tests/minicourse/lesson8a_cycle.fspy=: =pro= derives B
from an obligation for A, and =back= derives A from an obligation for B.
Unfolded for pro:

: unfold: B[t] expanded, root u1 (Goal), 1 supporter(s), 'pro' on top
: unfold:   B[t] +support 'pro' (the argument unfolded, on top)
: unfold: A[t] expanded, root u2 (Goal), 1 supporter(s)
: unfold:   A[t] +support 'back'
: unfold: B[t] captured as 'b1'
:
: μalt1:B.<?u1:B||μ'b1:B.<μ_:B.<b1:B||alt1:B>||μ'_:B.<μpro:B.<μth:B.<r1:A->B||μalt2:A.<?u2:A||μ'b2:A.<μ_:A.<b2:A||alt2:A>||μ'_:A.<μback:A.<μth_2:A.<r3:B->A||b1:B*th_2:A>||back:A>||alt2:A>>>*th:B>||pro:B>||alt1:B>>>

Inside back, the demand for B is the demand the whole debate is about:
an obligation for a statement already being proved. The support
scaffold of B binds B[t] to =b1=, so the demand becomes =b1=. After that
scaffold's outer step =b1= is B's root, so back shares the obligation
?u1 instead of opening a second one; evaluation leaves it open
(=μpro:B.<r1:A->B||μb1:A.<r3:B->A||?u1:B*b1:A>*pro:B>=).

*A capture, and the contrary's root.* =tests/rationality/self_attack_lk.fspy=:
one argument for P from =pRule: (P->false)->P=, whose body presumes "P

=SelfAttack=, and the default marker P[c]: presumption. Unfolded for
SelfAttack:

: unfold: P[t] expanded, root u1 (Goal), 1 supporter(s), 'SelfAttack' on top
: unfold:   P[t] +support 'SelfAttack' (the argument unfolded, on top)
: unfold: P[c] captured as 'rule' (presumption)
: unfold:   P[t] attacked by the debate about P[c], 0 supporter(s) (alt alt2, b b2)
: unfold: the bare P[c] captured as 'alt2'
: unfold: P[c], the attacker wing, root 'alt2'
:
: μalt2:P.<μalt1:P.<?u1:P||μ'b1:P.<μ_:P.<b1:P||alt1:P>||μ'_:P.<μSelfAttack:P.<μrule:P.<pRule:(P->false)->P||λh:P.μalpha:false.<h:P||rule:P>*rule:P>||SelfAttack:P>||alt1:P>>>||μ'b2:P.<μ_:P.<b2:P||alt2:P>||μ'_:P.<b2:P||alt2:P>>>

Inside the body, at the site for P[c], =env= is {P[c]: rule, P[t]: h,
false[c]: alpha}: =mu rule:P= is a continuation for P, so the
presumption "P fails" becomes =rule=. Only SelfAttack is a supporter;
its lambda edge is inside it. And because the document presumes P[c],
the issue is attacked by the debate about P[c]: nobody derives "P

* Lesson 9 - The input: a labelled, unfolded term

** Definition

The term is *closed* (every variable is bound), and Fellowship has
replayed it (the type check), so it is a well-typed proof term in which
some leaves are open. What it can contain:

| printed        | what it is                                                | who makes it     |
|----------------+-----------------------------------------------------------+------------------|
| =?u1:B=        | obligation site, term side (Goal)                         | unfold, =_site=  |
| =u1:A?=        | obligation site, context side (Laog)                      | unfold           |
| =!u2:A=        | presumption site, term side (Deleg)                       | unfold           |
| =u1:P!=        | presumption site, context side (Geled)                    | unfold           |
| =r1=, =nc=     | declared names: axioms, strict leaves                     | =declare=        |
| =alt1=, =alt2= | a scaffold's outer binder (alpha, or x on the context side) | =_fresh_alt=   |
| =b1=, =b2=     | a scaffold's wiring binder beta                           | unfold           |
| =_=            | the two affine binders of a scaffold's inner pair         | unfold           |
| =pro=, =con=   | an argument's own eta wrapper =mu pro.< ... ‖ pro >=      | the argument     |
| =th_2=, =H1_2= | an argument's own binder, suffixed on reuse               | unfold, =fresh=  |

In phase 2 the term also carries sigma, and its normal form has sites
named by their labels (since 2026-10-07, tasks.org
aida-labelled-sites-delegation-rewrite):

| printed             | what it is                                                     | who makes it      |
|---------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------+-------------------|
| =B{IN}=             | a term-sorted occurrence of B (mu, site, term variable), labelled | =label_term=    |
| ={OUT}A=            | a context-sorted occurrence of A (mu', site, continuation)     | =label_term=      |
| =!IN:A=, =IN:A!=    | a site labelled IN: a delegation, whatever it was              | =discharge_labels= |
| =?OUT:B=, =UNDEC:P?= | a site labelled OUT or UNDEC: an obligation, whatever it was  | =discharge_labels= |

Sites, =alt= and =b= binders each have their own counter. Lesson 8a
builds this term step by step.

Unfolding numbers the sites =u1=, =u2=, ... in the order it reaches them.
It keeps an argument's binder names, and suffixes one only if that name
is already used elsewhere in the term, because Fellowship refuses a
hypothesis introduced twice. (Since 2026-10-02 the unfolder's own names
=alt=, =b= and =x= are chosen the same way, from the names not yet taken:
an argument whose own binder is called =alt1= - a term pasted from an
earlier unfolding has one - no longer produces that name twice.)

*Is anything special about =alt=?* In evaluation, no. The matcher
(=_match_paper_scaffold=, =core/dc/debate_graph.py=) recognises the
scaffold's alpha by where it stands, not by its name: it is whatever the
outer binder is called, and the kept-wing checks compare against that
name. =alt= is only a naming convention. Three things *are* special:

  - *The name =_=.* It marks an affine binder, and the matcher tests
    affinity by that name (=_is_affine_binder=), not by checking that the
    variable is unused. Lesson 13 shows why this matters.
  - *alpha and beta bind, like any binder* (since 2026-10-07). While
    unfolding, a site is captured by the innermost binder in scope for
    its statement, an argument's own or a scaffold's: alpha binds the
    contrary of the scaffold's statement, beta the statement itself
    (lesson 8a). The strict phase does not count them as settled,
    though, as it does an argument's binders (lesson 10), and the
    rewrites substitute beta (lesson 13).
  - *alpha is eta-removed after a decision* when the kept wing does not
    use it (lesson 11). So =alt= names disappear from the result, and an
    argument's own wrapper name (=pro=) is what the normal form and the
    strict edges carry.

The literal string ="alt"= does matter in one place: the legacy
term-level reducer, =core/comp/reduce.py=, which =reduce= and =normalize=
still use (the =support= and =attack= verbs that grafted for it are gone).
Evaluation never calls it.

*sigma* maps each statement of the issue graph to IN, OUT or UNDEC.
Phase 1 reads strictness off the term and does not use it. Then sigma is
written into the term (=label_term=, =core/comp/labelled.py=): every
occurrence of a statement carries its label, =A{L}= on the term side and
={L}A= on the context side. The label depends only on the proposition
and the side, so no reduction step can make it wrong. Phase 2 reads the
labels off this term, through a scaffold's derivation status. Before
phase 3 they are discharged into the sites (lesson 12), and phase 3
reads nothing but the base strategy.

* Lesson 10 - Phase 1: strictness

** Definition

A subterm is *strict in the debate* iff it contains no open site: no
Goal, Laog, Deleg or Geled (=strict_in_debate=). Nothing else counts:

  - A *free variable* bound by an *argument's* binder is not an
    assumption. The whole term is closed, so such a variable is bound by
    some enclosing binder of an argument, a commitment the debate has
    already made. This covers sites captured by an argument (lesson 13).
  - A free variable bound by a *scaffold's* binder is not a commitment
    (since 2026-10-07, aida-unfold-scaffold-binders-capture). It is
    taken for what it stands for (=strict_in_scope=): a =b= for its
    supported term after its own strict decisions, substituted in a copy
    and judged with it (following such variables outward); an =alt= for
    "the statement fails", an open site. So a supporter that needs the
    statement it supports is strict only if what it supports is, and the
    attacker wing's captured root is never strict.
  - A *declared name* is a leaf with nothing open: strict.
  - A *clash* - an original facing its winning attacker under an unused
    binder - has no open site if neither side has one. It is strict. It
    decides a scaffold like any strict wing and then surfaces as an
    exception when the normal form is classified.

The rules, with t the supported or attacked term (the code calls it the
original), t2 the supporter and e2 the attacker:

| scaffold | condition                              | decision                   | result                  |
|----------+----------------------------------------+----------------------------+-------------------------|
| support  | both strict, t an uncaught contradiction | keep t                   | t                       |
| support  | else t2 strict                         | keep the supporter         | t2                      |
| support  | else t strict                          | keep t                     | t                       |
| support  | neither                                | delay                      | unchanged               |
| attack   | e2 strict, t not                       | defeat                     | =mu alpha.< t ‖ e2 >=   |
| attack   | t strict, e2 not                       | keep t                     | t                       |
| attack   | both, or neither                       | delay (both: inconsistent) | unchanged               |

*The supporter first* (since 2026-10-06, aida-unfold-entrypoints). In a
stack the supported term of an inner support scaffold is a lower
supporter. Checking the supporter first lets the higher of two strict
supporters win, so the strict phase keeps the stack's order, which sigma
keeps too (lesson 11). Outside a stack t is a site, never strict, and
the order changes nothing. *One exception* is its own piece of
semantics, not a consequence of the order (the author): among strict
terms an uncaught strict contradiction always wins. If t is strict but
holds a clash no binder in scope can catch, and t2 holds none, t is
kept, so that the contradiction surfaces as an exception wherever it
stands in the stack.

*Bottom-up.* =strict_resolve= recognises a scaffold top-down, rewrites
its supported (or attacked) term and its scion first, and only then
decides it. The order is
necessary: deciding an inner scaffold can remove an open site, and that
can make an outer wing strict. In the self-attack
(=tests/rationality/self_attack_lk.fspy=) the root's support scaffold
contains the obligation ?u1; only after its decision ("supporter
strict") has dropped ?u1 is the whole term closed, and only then is it a
strict edge (=SelfAttack*=). (Before 2026-10-07 the attack around it was
then decided too, "original strict"; its wing is now the captured
contrary alone, which is no scaffold, lesson 8a.)

*Strict edges.* After deciding, =strict_resolve= collects every
outermost eta-wrapped subterm =mu n.< t || n >= that
  1. contains a strictly decided scaffold,
  2. is *closed* - strict, and no free variable except declared names - and
  3. holds no uncatchable clash.
Each such subterm becomes a source-less strict edge =n*= of the issue
graph: a strict derivation the framework could not see. Condition 3
exists because a subterm that holds a clash derives its statement only
from an inconsistency.

*How strict subterms are handled, end to end.* A strict argument in the
document unfolds to its body, like killC here, and is strict because it
has no site. A strict argument used by =axiom NAME= or =cite NAME= is a
declared-name leaf. Phase 1 decides every scaffold that strictness
decides. Its strict edges enter the issue graph *before* labelling, so
sigma cannot contradict a strict decision. After normalisation,
=check_conservativity= requires that an input with no site at all
normalises to a term with no open obligation.

*Phase 1 runs twice.* =compile_issue= runs it to collect the strict
edges for the labelling and discards the rewritten term.
=_evaluate_under= then runs it again to obtain that term. The results
are identical. (On the shared route it runs once per sub-debate and the
result is kept; minicourse-sharing.org, lesson 17.)

** Example

=explain pro.=, phase 1:

: strict  C[c] supporter strict
: strict  C[c] attacker strict: defeat
: strict  A[c] delayed for the labelling (neither the original nor the supporter is strict)
: strict  A[c] delayed for the labelling (neither wing is strict)
: strict  B[t] delayed for the labelling (neither the original nor the supporter is strict)
: strict  no edge for 'pro' on B[t]: not closed (an open site remains)
: strict  no edge for 'con' on A[c]: not closed (an open site remains)
: strict  edge 'killC*' on C[c] (a closed derivation the framework missed)

(The log names a scaffold by the statement its scion concludes: both C
lines are about killC's refutation of C, both A lines about con's.)

The debate about C is innermost, so it is decided first, in two steps.
Its wing CW is the root =alt4= supported by K; K has no site, and =alt4=
is the attack's =alt=, which stands for "C fails" and counts as open, so
the supporter wins, and the wing becomes K. Now CSC has the presumption
!u3 as its attacked term and K as its attacker; !u3 is a site and K

: μalt4:C.<!u3:C||μ'killC:C.<killC:C||μ'x_2:C.<nc:~C||μ'H1:~C.<H1:~C||x_2:C*_F_>>>>

that is, =mu alt4:C.< !u3 || K >= with =alt4= unused. The defeat leaves
!u3 in place, so CON still rests on a site, and neither the debate about
A[c] (its root =alt2=, open, and CON) nor the A scaffold can be decided
by strictness. PRO contains ASC, which contains !u2, so neither can the
B scaffold. Had =alt2= counted as settled, as an argument's binder does,
the wing AW would have been "original strict" and con dropped before
sigma ever saw it.

The first decision gives a strict edge. K, killC's own wrapper, is
closed and is the term kept by a decision, so the collector records
=killC*: C[c] <- -=. It changes no label: killC is already a strict edge
of the framework. Before 2026-10-06 the term had K itself as the
attacker of the presumption of C, with no scaffold decided inside or
around K's wrapper,
and no =killC*= was collected.

context-side scaffold; =fire= at the binder =alt5=):

: W1  (mu<)  < mu b5.< mu _.<alt5||K> || mu'_.<alt5||b5> > || alt4 >
:              -->  < mu _.<alt5||K> || mu'_.<alt5||alt4> >

:              -->  < alt5 || K >                             [decided: the supporter]

then the attack (=scaffold_at(term, "u3")=):

:              -->  < mu _.<!u3||K> || mu'_.<!u3||alt4> >

* Lesson 11 - Phase 2: sigma

** Definition

=resolve_scaffolds= walks the term *top-down*. At each scaffold it
still finds:

1. *Compile the scion* against the binders in scope (=scion_record=).
   This gives the edge the compiler would give the scion: its target
   statement and its sources. A scion that is a *stack* - the supporter
   stack under a root, or an attacker wing (lesson 8a) - stands for
   several alternatives and gives one edge per element
   (=stack_elements=); a lone root counts as an identity edge, the
   statement resting on itself. A root captured by the attack's =alt=
   compiles the same way: the captured variable is a source at the
   statement it is bound to. The stack's own =alt= and =b= are in scope
   for each element (=scoped_stack_elements=).
2. *Derivation status* under sigma (=derivation_status=): for one edge,
   IN if the target and every source are IN, OUT if any of them is OUT,
   and UNDEC otherwise; for several, IN if some alternative is IN, OUT
   if all are OUT. So a stack is IN if some supporter in it is. The
   labels are those of the labelled term (lesson 9): the compiler copies
   each occurrence's label into the edge it builds - the target's from
   the scion's root, a source's from its site, captured variable or
   lambda. sigma itself is not consulted again.
3. *Wing choice* (=_wing_choice=): the table of lesson 7. The mode's
   tiebreak is used only when the status is UNDEC.
4. *Rewrite* the scaffold to the kept wing - =keep_orig=,
   =keep_scion_support= or =keep_attack_wing=, the same functions phase 1
   uses - and walk on *into the result only*.

Step 4 has a consequence. Every scaffold inside a discarded wing is
never consulted, which is how one decision can remove a whole subdebate.
The =sigma= log line counts what went with it.

*The steps behind one rewrite.* A term-side scaffold
=mu alpha.< t || mu'beta.< W1 || W2 > >= becomes its kept wing in three
steps:

| step       | rule                         | what it does                                        |
|------------+------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------|
| outer pair | (>mu)                        | beta := t, the original enters both wings - and the kept wing, wherever it uses beta |
| inner pair | (mu<) or (>mu) - the decision | keeps one wing, discards the other                  |
| eta        | not a reduction rule          | =mu alpha.< u ‖ alpha >= to u, when alpha is not free in u |

Which rule means what depends on the scaffold (lesson 6):

| scaffold (term side) | (mu<) keeps                   | (>mu) keeps  |
|----------------------+-------------------------------+--------------|
| support              | the original                  | the supporter |
| attack               | the attacker, facing t (defeat) | the original |

The context side is the mirror image.

** Example

=explain pro.=, phase 2:

: sigma  supporter B[t] is IN -> keep the supporter, dropping the original
: sigma  attacker A[c] is OUT -> keep the original, dropping the attacker and the 1 scaffold(s) inside it

The "IN" and "OUT" in these lines are *derivation statuses*, not the
labels of B[t] and A[c]. The B scaffold's scion is PRO, whose edge is
B[t] <- A[t]:pres@u2, and B[t] and A[t] are both IN, so its status is
IN. The A scaffold's scion is the wing AW, with two alternatives: its
root =alt2=, recorded as an obligation source at A[c] - the identity
edge A[c] <- A[c] - and con, A[c] <- C[t]:pres@u3.
A[c] is OUT, so both are OUT, and so is AW. Walking top-down, phase 2
decides B, keeps PRO, walks into it, and decides A. It never reaches
anything inside AW: the support scaffold there (the "1 scaffold inside
it") and the debate about C go with it, and phase 1's decisions on C
were work for nothing. The result, still labelled:

: μpro:B{IN}.<μth:B{IN}.<r1:A->B||!u2:A{IN}*th:B>||pro:B>

*The same two decisions as reduction steps:*

: B1  (>mu)  < ?u1 || mu'b1.< mu _.<b1||alt1> || mu'_.<PRO||alt1> > >
:              -->  < mu _.<?u1||alt1> || mu'_.<PRO||alt1> >
: B2  (>mu)  < mu _.<?u1||alt1> || mu'_.<PRO||alt1> >
:              -->  < PRO || alt1 >                           [decided: supporter IN]
: B3  eta    mu alt1.< PRO || alt1 >  -->  PRO

:              -->  < mu _.<!u2||AW> || mu'_.<!u2||alt2> >

gives =c[pro := alt1]=, the same term with =pro= renamed to =alt1=. A3

applies to it. Only eta removes =alt2=. If A3 is left out, the steps
lead to a different normal form (lesson 12).

*A stack, read top-down.* In =tests/entrypoints.fspy= B has three
supporters, all IN. The canonical term =SUP( ?u1, SUP( SUP(P1, P2), P3 ) )=
is decided as: the root scaffold's scion is the whole stack, IN because
some element is, so the stack is kept; then P3 is IN, so P3 is kept and
the rest dropped. =evaluate issue :B.= returns p3's proof. In p1's term
=SUP( ?u1, SUP( SUP(P2, P3), P1 ) )= the same walk keeps P1 first. Had
P1's derivation been OUT, the walk would go on to P3, then P2, and only
then to the root.

*Where sigma differs from the base strategy.* Plain =normalize_strong=
on the unfolded term, with no sigma at all:

: cbn:  μalt1:B.<r1:A->B||μb1:A.<!u2:A||b1:A>*alt1:B>
: cbv:  μalt1:B.<?u1:B||alt1:B>

decides. It therefore reaches sigma's answer up to eta and renaming.

* Lesson 12 - Phase 3: normalisation and the class

** Definition

*Discharging the labels* comes first (=discharge_labels=,
=core/comp/labelled.py=). Every site takes its label as its name, and
its kind from the label, whatever kind it had:

| label        | obligation =?u:A=  | delegation =!u:A=  | (context side: the mirror) |
|--------------+--------------------+--------------------+----------------------------|
| IN           | =!IN:A=            | =!IN:A=            | =IN:A!=                    |
| OUT          | =?OUT:A=           | =?OUT:A=           | =OUT:A?=                   |
| UNDEC        | =?UNDEC:A=         | =?UNDEC:A=         | =UNDEC:A?=                 |

An obligation labelled IN is a delegation: the onus is now on the
opponent, who would have to bring an argument the labelling has not
considered. A delegation not IN is an obligation again: it was not
established under the mode. All other labels are dropped, so the
normaliser, alpha-equality and the matcher never see one. A site's
name no longer says which site it was; in a normal form it does not
need to.

=normalize_strong= applies the four standard rules of the calculus -
(->), (-), (mu<), (>mu) - under every congruence. It works one step at a
time: each step fires at the leftmost-outermost command that has a redex
(term slot before context slot) and then restarts from the root. The
only choice the rules leave open is at a critical pair =< mu a.c || mu'x.c' >=.
There, cbv fires (mu<) first and cbn fires (>mu) first. It runs out of
fuel after 2000 steps and raises rather than returning a half-reduced
term.

After phases 1 and 2 no scaffold is left, so the critical pairs the base
strategy meets are ordinary cuts in the arguments' own proofs. On a
debate whose scaffolds are all decided, the base strategy can still
change the normal form, but only through those cuts.

=classify_nf= then reports, checking in this order:

  - =exception= if the term holds an uncatchable clash: an affine mu/mu'
    whose command no enclosing mu/mu' binder can catch. This is checked
    first (since 2026-10-07, tasks.org
    aida-classify-exception-before-open): an exception is an exception
    whether the defeated site is an obligation or a presumption, and
    whatever obligations remain elsewhere. Lesson 9 with con's
    =by default.= removed gives =μalt1:C.<nc:~C||?u1:C*_F_>= for
    =issue :C=: the obligation for C is strictly refuted, an exception.
    Until then an obligation anywhere hid the clash, and this was open;
  - =open= if an obligation (Goal/Laog) remains;
  - =value= otherwise. Presumptions may remain; the value is then a
    proof modulo them.

After discharge, the last two read: a value iff every remaining site is
IN. Issue =P:= of tests/demo/05_even_loop.fspy - the opponent's onus to
refute P, delegated by a presumption - is UNDEC skeptically, so the
delegation is the obligation =UNDEC:P?= and the debate is open
(=μ'alt1:P.<alt1:P||UNDEC:P?>=; a value, =μ'alt1:P.<alt1:P||u1:P!>=,
before 2026-10-07). Credulously the witness has P[c] IN: =IN:P!=, a
value.

** Example

: normalise  normal form after 1 step(s) under cbn
: N1  (mu<)  < mu th.< r1 || !IN * th > || pro >  -->  < r1 || !IN * pro >

Discharge turns =!u2:A{IN}= into =!IN:A=. The root command has a mu on
the term side and the variable =pro= on the context side, so only (mu<)
applies. Afterwards =< r1 || !IN * pro >= is normal: (->) would need a
lambda, and =r1= is a declared name. The result, in both modes and under
both base strategies:

: μpro:B.<r1:A->B||!IN:A*pro:B>          VALUE

This is a proof of B from =r1=, modulo the presumption of A. sigma says
nobody defeated A, so A stays presumed, and its label is IN.

* Lesson 13 - Names, capture and substitution

** Definition

Four different things in the pipeline involve binders and names. It
helps to keep them apart.

*1. Capture at unfolding, on purpose.* While expanding an argument's
body, the unfolder keeps the binders of the enclosing bodies in scope:
a lambda's hypothesis and a mu's or mu''s variable, each standing for a
statement. A site whose statement is bound in scope becomes that
variable. This is how a cycle is represented in a finite term. The
self-attack: =pRule: ((P->false)->P)=, and the body's presumption
"P fails", =u:P!=, sits under =mu rule:P=, which is the continuation for
P:

: unfold  P[c] captured as 'rule' (presumption)
: ... μrule:P.<pRule:(P->false)->P||λh:P.μalpha:false.<h:P||rule:P>*rule:P> ...

The site is gone, so the supporter is strict (lesson 10). The debate
keeps the presumption as a *source* in the framework (marked
=captured_presumption=), so the labelling still sees the cycle. Since
2026-10-07 the scaffold binders =alt= and =b= are in this scope too
(lesson 8a): a demand the innermost binder for its statement is a
scaffold's is captured by that =alt= or =b=. Unlike an argument's
binder, the strict phase does not count those as settled (lesson 10).

*2. Renaming at unfolding.* The same argument body can be unfolded more
than once, and different arguments reuse names such as =x=, =th= and
=H1=. The unfolder keeps a name the first time it meets it and suffixes
later ones (=th_2=, =H1_2=), because Fellowship refuses a hypothesis
introduced twice. This is renaming, not capture avoidance: the names
must be globally distinct for the replay.

*3. Capture-avoiding substitution in the normaliser.* (mu<) and (>mu)
substitute, and =substitute= avoids capture the simple way. Before
replacing, it renames every binder of the target to a fresh =bN=
(=freshen_binders=) - every binder except the placeholder =_=, which
binds nothing and so can capture nothing (since 2026-10-06; before, =_=
was renamed too). Here is the real C1 step, taken on the unfolded term,
with the wing CW still undecided:

: μalt4:C.<μ_:C.<!u3:C||μ'b1:C.<μb2:C.<μ_:C.<b1:C||μ'b3:C.<b3:C||μ'b6:C.<nc:~C||μ'b7:~C.<b7:~C||b6:C*_F_>>>>||μ'_:C.<b1:C||b2:C>>||alt4:C>>||μ'_:C.<!u3:C||alt4:C>>

The lesson 10 account prints =mu _.<!u3||CW>=, and so does the real
term now. Inside the wing =alt5= and =b5= became =b1= and =b2=, and
=killC=, =x_2= and =H1= inside K became =b3=, =b6= and =b7= (=b4= and =b5=
are taken: the fresh names avoid every name of the term); the =_=
binders stayed. Three things follow:

  - Normal forms carry =bN= names wherever a substitution passed through
    a binder. The self-attack's normal form is
    =μalt2:P.<pRule||λb1:P.μb2:false.<b1:P||alt2:P>*alt2:P>=: =h= and
    =alpha= were renamed by a (mu<) step, and the root binder is the
    attack's =alt2= (below, "eta and a captured alpha"). In the running
    example the one normalisation step went through no binder, so its
    normal form keeps its names.
  - The renaming goes further than capture avoidance needs. At C1 the
    replacement =!u3= has no names at all, so nothing could be captured,
    yet every binder is renamed. It is correct, only more than necessary.
    The scaffold rewrites (point 4) use the minimal form,
    =substitute(..., minimal=True)=, which renames only the binders that
    could capture, so the arguments' names survive them.
  - *After a real step, the matcher still finds the scaffolds inside.*
    It recognises affine binders by the name =_= (lesson 9), and the
    substitution keeps that name: in the step above, the wing CW is
    still recognised as the support scaffold on C[c]. Until 2026-10-06
    =_= was renamed as well, and every scaffold a substitution passed
    through became unrecognisable; that was the concrete reason phases 1
    and 2 rewrite whole scaffolds instead of firing the steps. It is
    one obstacle less for doing them as steps (last section).

*4. The scaffold rewrites substitute beta.* The outer step substitutes
the original t for beta. In the wiring beta occurs once in each wing, at
the top level of that wing's command, under nothing but the wing's own
=_=; there t could simply be copied. But since beta captures (lesson
8a), it can also occur *inside* the scion: a supporter that needs the
statement it supports, or an attacker wing's own supporters. The kept
wing then still mentions beta, and dropping beta's binder would leave it
free (the compiler refused such terms: "free term variable 'b7'").
=keep_scion_support= and =keep_attack_wing= therefore take the outer
step for real, =scion[b := t]= (=_outer_step=, minimal substitution),
before they keep the wing. =keep_orig= keeps t itself, in which beta
cannot occur (t is outside beta's scope). In the capture-by-b example of
lesson 8a, keeping back puts B's root ?u1 where back had =b1=.

*eta and a captured alpha.* A rewrite removes alpha by eta only when the
kept wing does not use it (=_eta=). A wing in which alpha occurs free
keeps its =mu alpha= wrapper. Since alpha captures (lesson 8a), this
arises on the paper shapes too, wherever a kept wing holds a captured
alpha; it also arose in the legacy shapes the removed grafting verbs
built. And an
attack whose wing is nothing but its captured root, as in the
self-attack, is no scaffold at all: normalisation reduces it, and its
=alt2= ends up as the binder at the root of the normal form, eta-equal
to the proof itself (=μSelfAttack=), the same under cbn and cbv up to
that one eta step. The eta step also decides the names. Without
it the result is =mu alt1.< PRO || alt1 >=, whose eta wrapper is named
=alt1=. The strict-edge collector names an edge after that wrapper, so it
would call the edge =alt1*= instead of =pro*=.

** Example

Replaying lesson 11's steps with the real rules, and taking (mu<) instead
of eta at B3:

: after B1, B2, B3=(mu<), A1, A2, then normalize_strong cbn:
: μalt1:B.<r1:A->B||μb1:A.<!u2:A||b1:A>*alt1:B>
: the pipeline:
: μpro:B.<r1:A->B||!IN:A*pro:B>

The two differ by one eta step (A3), by the name of the root binder, and
by the site's name: the steps by hand skip the labels. The test labels
and discharges the hand-made normal form with the same sigma and then
checks that the two are equal once both are eta-reduced. They are not
alpha-equal.

** Point 1 - Against a bare default, a defeasible argument defeats as a strict one does

* What this means for a single label-guided normaliser

The lessons show that phases 1 and 2 *are* normalisation. What stands
between the implementation and doing them as normalisation steps:

1. *Affinity by name* - solved 2026-10-06: =freshen_binders= leaves =_=
   alone, so a scaffold stays recognisable after a substitution (lesson
   13). Before, substitution renamed =_=, and the matcher lost the
   scaffold after one step.
2. *eta is not a rule.* Without it, stepwise normal forms keep
   =mu alt.< !u || alt >= residues and differ from today's (lessons 11,
   13). A stepwise normaliser needs eta for the scaffold's alpha, and
   only for alpha: the arguments' own wrappers must stay, because they
   name strict edges.
3. *Outer pairs are not always free of choice.* In this example every
   supported or attacked term is a leaf, so the outer command has one
   rule. When it is a mu-term - an argument lower in a stack (lesson
   8a), or the support scaffold an attack wraps - the outer command is
   itself a critical pair. The rewrites always resolve it by (>mu)
   (beta := the original), whatever the base strategy; since 2026-10-07
   they take that step for real, substituting beta in the kept wing
   (lesson 13, point 4), and the strict phase judges a captured beta by
   the same substitution (lesson 10). A stepwise normaliser must make
   the same choice explicitly, or the nested scaffold loses its shape.
4. *The phases walk in opposite orders.* Strictness is decided
   bottom-up, because an inner decision can make an outer wing strict
   (lesson 10). Sigma is decided top-down, so discarded subdebates are
   never visited. Leftmost-outermost normalisation is top-down, so
   strict decisions need an inner-first pass or a rule for reducing
   inner pairs first.
5. *Phase 1 runs twice*, and it decides scaffolds that phase 2 then
   discards (lesson 10). On the shared route it runs once per
   sub-debate, but still before sigma, so the second half stands.
6. *sigma is in the term* (since 2026-10-07). Phase 2 reads the labels
   off the labelled term (lessons 9, 11), and a label depends only on a
   statement, so every reduction step keeps the labels right. In a
   single normaliser they are type annotations, and discharging them
   (lesson 12) is a local pass that commutes with the rules: it can come
   at any point after the last decision.
