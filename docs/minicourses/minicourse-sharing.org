* About this sequel

minicourse-evaluation.org describes how a debate is unfolded from the
document graph into one term (lesson 8a) and follows that term through
evaluation (lessons 9-13). Both describe the *unfolded* route. This
sequel describes the other one, the *shared* route, which is the CLI
default.

*Note (2026-10-16).* The shared route builds what the unfolded route
builds: the stacked shape of lesson 8a - the root under one support
scaffold whose scion is the supporter stack, one attack scaffold whose
scion is the contrary's debate - and the argument entrypoints, an
argument's debate with the argument on top of its stack. Until this date
it built an older, *legacy* shape and was compared with a legacy
unfolding; both are gone. It also exposes every horn of an argument
(minicourse-evaluation.org, lesson 8a; tasks.org, aida-rational-closure):
a declared axiom applied, a lemma, a lambda becomes, where it stands, the
debate about its statement, as soon as some other argument derives,
contests or presumes that statement. In the fixtures below the two
arguments at each level apply *different* axioms with the same statement
(=r1_0= and =r1_1=, both =P0 -> P1=), and in lesson 15 two arguments
prove the same lambda. Each such statement is therefore engaged, and its
debate is a sub-debate of its own, shared like any other.

The reason is size. Unfolding copies a statement's debate into every
place the statement is needed, so the term is as large as the number of
paths through the document graph. With two alternative supporters per
level, the number of paths doubles per level:

| levels | definitions | shared form (nodes) | unfolded term (nodes) |
|--------+-------------+---------------------+-----------------------|
|      1 |           3 |                  47 |                    61 |
|      2 |           5 |                  93 |                   181 |
|      3 |           7 |                 139 |                   421 |
|      4 |           9 |                 185 |                   901 |
|      8 |          17 |                 369 |                 15301 |

(measured on the issue P_n of the generated documents of
tests/test_share.py; the term follows size(n) = 2 size(n-1) + 59, so
sixteen levels give about 3.9 million nodes; two definitions per level,
the statement and its implication). Before the sharing work, =label= on
ten levels - twenty arguments - took 105 seconds. Now it takes about one.

The pipeline on the default route:

:   document graph, issue
:     |  share               core/dc/share.py         one definition per debate, citations
:     |  typecheck_shared    core/dc/typecheck.py     replay each definition once
:     |  IssueResolver       core/dc/instances.py     resolve each INSTANCE once (its strict edges)
:     |  labellings          core/comp/adf_label.py   as before, on the same issue graph
:     |  evaluate_shared     core/comp/evaluate.py    sigma opens a sub-debate only where it is kept
:     v  normalize_strong, classify_nf                as before

Four decisions shape it (author, 2026-10-01):

1. *Names are transparent.* A named sub-debate means its expansion;
   nothing new enters the calculus.
2. *One debate per statement*, that is, per (proposition, side). Since
   the stacked shape, read: one debate per statement *and variant*
   (lesson 14) - the unfolder builds a different debate about the same
   statement where an argument is on top of its stack or a root has
   another kind.
3. *A name is shown from the second occurrence on*; a sub-debate used
   once stays in place.
4. *Capture does not force copying.* A citation records what its site
   does to the cited debate.

And one about what may be memoised: not a definition, but an *instance*
(lesson 17). The lemma "normalise the definition once, then plug it in"
is false, since strictness is read off the concrete term and the same
sub-debate is decided differently under different captures.

The unfolded route remains as the reference the shared one is tested
against: =pipeline unfolded.= (or =FSP_PIPELINE=unfolded=) selects it,
=pipeline shared.= this one. Against the unfolding the shared route
gives the same term when expanded, the same graph, labels and normal
form, up to the names of binders and the numbers of sites.

Prerequisites: lesson 2 (hyperedges), lesson 7 (sigma), and lessons 8a,
10, 11 and 13 of the first sequel (unfolding, the strict phase, sigma's
walk, capture).

* Lesson 14 - Definitions, citations and variants

** Definition

=share(graph, issue)= (=core/dc/share.py=) builds one *definition* for
every debate the issue reaches: the debate the unfolder's =_debate=
builds for a statement - its root, under one support scaffold whose
scion is the supporter stack, and the attack scaffold whose scion is the
contrary's debate - with the unfolder's own code. The difference is what
happens at a site inside an edge's body. The unfolder expands the site's
statement in place, in scope. =share= leaves a *citation* there: a leaf
that names the debate and will be expanded later, if at all.

A citation is a term- or context-side name leaf (=§= internally) that
records what it cites, whether the site was a presumption, and what the
site's scope does to the cited debate (lesson 15). A statement whose
definition is a bare site - nobody derives or contests it - is *trivial*
and is written in place as that site.

*Variants.* The unfolder does not always build the same debate about a
statement. An argument's term puts the argument on top of its stack, and
the sites in its own body keep the kind it wrote (minicourse-evaluation.org,
lesson 8a); an exposed node's debate has an obligation root, a
presumption of the statement at the bottom of the stack, and the node on
top when its argument has primacy. Each of these is a *variant*: a
definition keyed by (statement, root kind, argument on top), next to the
statement's canonical one. A citation names the definition it means.

*Expansion.* =SharedDebate.expand()= writes every definition back in,
interpreting each citation as the unfolder would: a captured statement
becomes the capturing variable, a statement already being expanded on
the path becomes a bare site, any other becomes the definition's body
with its binders renamed on reuse. The result is the unfolded term up to
alpha-equivalence and site numbering, and that equation is the gate the
whole route is tested against: =expand= must agree with =unfold= (and,
for an argument, =unfold_argument=) on every fixture, and does.

*Names.* A definition is shown by name when it is cited from two or more
sites along the root's expansion, or when the author cited it with
=cite NAME=. The name of a canonical debate is the registered argument
or debate whose issue is the statement, if there is exactly one;
otherwise - and for a variant - =anon_1=, =anon_2=, ..., numbered on
first use and kept with the document, so the numbers do not shift
between commands. =anon_= is a reserved prefix. A sub-debate needed once
is printed in place, and a trivial one is never named.

*What =share ARG.= prints:* the debate biased towards ARG - the one
=evaluate ARG.= evaluates - in which every named sub-debate is a
citation, and then one line

: NAME[open sites] := body

per named definition. The header lists the sites the sub-debate rests on
when nothing outside captures them.

* Lesson 15 - Captures and cuts

** Definition

The unfolder does three different things at a site, depending on its
scope (lesson 13): a statement bound by an enclosing binder becomes that
variable (*captured*); a statement already being expanded on the current
path stays a bare site (*cut*); any other is expanded. A definition is
built with an empty scope, so inside it only its own binders capture. A
citation therefore has to carry what the unfolder would have known at
that site: =captures=, a map from statements to the binder names that
capture them, and =cuts=, the statements on the path. Expansion
accumulates them from the outside in, inner ones overriding, and applies
the unfolder's three cases in the unfolder's order: captured first, then
cut, then expanded.

In the stacked shape every binder captures, the scaffolds' own included
(minicourse-evaluation.org, lesson 13): a support scaffold's =b= binds
its statement over the stack, and an attack's =alt= the contrary over
its wing. So a citation inside a stack records the scaffold's =b= as a
capture of the statement being supported, and capture alone ends the
unfolding; the cuts remain for the record.

Printed, a citation shows that information in brackets, in the author's
notation:

: d[alpha -> !:A, B:?]

reads "d, with alpha capturing its delegation of A, and its obligation B
left open": an arrow marks a captured statement and names the capturing
binder; a bare marker is a cut. The markers are the term printer's:
=?:A= and =!:A= for an obligation and a presumption on the term side,
=A:?= and =A:!= on the context side. A citation of a sub-debate that
nothing captures or cuts shows no bracket at all, as =anon_1= in lesson
14.

A capture can hit *any* statement the sub-debate reaches, not only a
leaf. In the even loop, "P fails" inside q is P's own continuation, and
the whole debate about "P fails" collapses to that variable. That is why
capture does not force copying: the citation says what collapses, and
expansion collapses it.

*A definition is larger than any of its uses.* Because a definition is
built without a scope, it expands statements that every citation of it
captures. The part it expands is dead at each such site: expansion puts
the variable there, not the body. What is actually compiled and resolved
is never the definition but an *instance* of it, the definition under a
given set of captures and cuts (lesson 17).

* Lesson 16 - The type check, one definition at a time

** Definition

The type check (lesson 9: Fellowship replays the term and the two shapes
are compared) replayed the unfolded term, and so grew with it. Now it
replays each definition once. The unit is the definition's *skeleton*:
its body with every citation left as the open site of the cited
statement, so a cited sub-debate is simply a hole of the right type.

This is sound because of what expansion puts at a citation: the cited
definition's body, which is a term or context of the statement's type; a
variable bound for that statement; or the statement's bare site. All
three have the type of the hole. So if every skeleton is well-typed,
every expansion is.

=typecheck_shared= (=core/dc/typecheck.py=) replays the skeletons of all
reachable definitions, skipping the trivial ones, and remembers what
replayed in the document (=prover.typechecked()=): declarations only
grow, so a skeleton that type-checked once still does, and a later
=label= on the same document replays nothing it has seen.
=typecheck expanded.= (or =FSP_TYPECHECK=expanded=) selects the old
whole-term replay, which is the reference the per-definition check is
tested against; both accept and reject the same documents. Since the
port the skeletons are stacked definitions, so the check replays the
shape that is evaluated.

*One statement, two spellings.* The graph identifies =~Q= with
=Q -> false= (=canonical_prop=, lesson 2); Fellowship does not. When one
argument needs =~Q= and another derives =Q -> false=, the unfolded term
holds a proof of =Q -> false= where =~Q= is required, and the replay is
refused when the scaffold throws back to its catch variable. No skeleton
contains that join - the site is open there - so the per-definition check
alone would accept a debate the whole-term replay refuses.
=SharedDebate.spelling_clashes()= detects a statement spelled in more
than one way among the arguments it reaches, and the CLI then replays the
expanded term instead. The refusal is an old one, recorded as
aida-negation-spelling-in-unfolding; the fallback keeps it as it was.

* Lesson 17 - Instances: the issue graph and the strict phase without the term

** Definition

An *instance* is a definition together with the statements captured and
cut in it - restricted to what its statement can reach - and it is the
unit the compiler and the strict phase work on. Two citations of the
same definition under the same captures and cuts, like the four in
lesson 15, are one instance: the binders' names are parameters, written
=§= internally, not part of the key. Where nothing is captured, there is
exactly one instance per definition.

Why not one definition? Strictness is read off the concrete term (lesson
10). A sub-debate whose only site is captured is strict; the same
sub-debate with that site open is not. So the same definition is decided
differently under different captures, and what can be memoised is the
instance. In the stacked shape what captures matters as well: a
statement captured by an argument's binder is a commitment the debate
made, one captured by an attack's =alt= stands for an open site, and one
captured by a scaffold's =b= is as strict as the original =b= stands for
(minicourse-evaluation.org, lesson 13). The strict phase therefore
chooses the instance where it meets the citation, under the scaffold
variables in scope there; such a choice is a variant of the instance for
the strict phase alone, and is not counted as an instance.

=IssueResolver= (=core/dc/instances.py=) does two things with
instances.

*Compile.* An instance's term is its definition's body with each
citation replaced by what the unfolder would put there: the capturing
variable, a bare site for a cut statement, or - for a cited instance - a
citation leaf that the compiler reads as a *source* of the cited
statement, of the kind the cited debate's root has. Meeting that leaf,
the compiler first compiles the cited instance (a hook on the compiler's
citation branch), so the edges come out in the order the unfolded term
would give them, each once. A cited debate whose root is no scaffold the
compiler recognises (an attack whose wing is only its own =alt=) is read
by the compiler as a sub-argument of the body it stands in; the
instance is written out there one level instead. The issue graph was the
union until 2026-10-15. Since then the issue graph is the part of the
document graph reachable from the issue (minicourse-evaluation.org,
lesson 8a), the instances contribute only their strict edges, and the
instance compiler is called from Python only
(=IssueResolver(shared, names).compile(name)=).

*Resolve.* =strict_resolve= runs on an instance's term in which each
cited instance is a *stub*: a chain of leaves holding exactly what an
enclosing scaffold may ask of the resolved instance - an open site if
one remains in it, the captured variables still free in it, and whether
a strict decision was made inside. The strict phase's own predicates
(open sites, free names, decisions) read a stub like any subterm, so a
scaffold is decided here exactly as it is on the unfolded term. Cited
instances are resolved where the strict phase meets them, and memoised.

*Strict edges* are collected across stubs, as the collector of lesson 10
collects them: the outermost closed copies of arguments defeasible as
registered. Only such a subterm is written out in full, because =adopt=
replays it. =fold_occurrences= then has nothing to fold: where the
unfolded term gave one edge per copy (a1_0 and a1_0_2), the instance
gives one.

* Lesson 18 - Evaluation without unfolding, and the two routes

** Definition

=evaluate_shared= (=core/comp/evaluate.py=) runs the pipeline of the
first sequel on the shared debate. Phase 1 is the per-instance strict
phase of lesson 17, already done when the issue graph was built. Phase 2
is =resolve_scaffolds=, walking top-down from the root's resolved term
as before, with one addition: when the walk reaches a stub, it writes
the cited instance out one level - its resolved term, under the names
the captured variables have at that site, still holding stubs for what
it cites in turn. The walk only reaches a stub inside a wing it keeps.
A dropped wing is never written out at all.

To decide a scaffold, phase 2 judges its scion by what it stands for
(minicourse-evaluation.org, lesson 11). A stub stands for its resolved
instance, judged under what its captured variables stand for at the
site; and where the compiler must read a scion's sources, a stub is a
source of its statement when the resolved instance still has the
statement's own site as its innermost original, and is opened one level
and read on otherwise.

After phase 2 the labels are discharged into the sites, so a site's name
is its label and an instance written out in two places brings its sites
twice with the same names; normalisation follows as before. What is normalised has the size of the
answer, not of the debate.

*The two routes.* =pipeline shared.= selects this route, the default;
=pipeline unfolded.= (or =FSP_PIPELINE=unfolded=) makes =graph=, =label=
and =evaluate= build the unfolded term and run the first sequel's
pipeline on it. The two give the same graph, labels, normal form and
class, for an issue and for an argument, =favour= included; what differs
is the names of binders and the numbers of sites, which the unfolded
route takes from the order of unfolding. On every statement and every
argument of every fixture, in both modes and under both base strategies,
the two routes have been compared and agree. If the shared route ever
fails other than by a refusal, the CLI warns and runs the unfolded one.

*Limits that remain.* The walks recurse: a chain of 90 arguments is
refused by =evaluate= and one of 150 by =label=, with one line
(aida-deep-term-recursion). The multi-extension semantics are as slow as
before: adf-bdd takes seconds for four independent even loops under
complete semantics (aida-multi-extension-labelling-cost). On small
debates the shared route is no faster than the unfolded one.

* What this means for the specification

debate-graph-spec.org describes unfolding as the bridge from the graph
to evaluation (the Layer 2->4 interface). That bridge is now the
reference route, and the production route computes the same things
without the term. Four notions deserve a place in the spec:

  - the *shared form*: one definition per debate, and citations with
    captures and cuts;
  - the *variant*: the debate about a statement the unfolder builds with
    an argument on top or a root of another kind, a definition of its
    own;
  - the *instance*: a definition with the statements captured and cut in
    it, the unit at which strictness is read off;
  - *lazy opening*: sigma writes a sub-debate out only inside a wing it
    keeps, so a lost sub-debate costs nothing.

Open after the sharing work (tasks.org): listing a document's statements
and arguments from the CLI, and naming an anonymous sub-debate
(aida-cli-inventory-and-naming); the two spellings of a negation
(aida-negation-spelling-in-unfolding); the recursion limits
(aida-deep-term-recursion); sharing composed debates, which are still
evaluated on their unfolded term; and the fallback to the unfolded
route, which should go once the shared route has seen real documents.
